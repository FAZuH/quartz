# Minggu 1

## Review: Probability and Random Variables

- **Random Variable**: A variable that takes on its values by chance.
- **Discrete RV**: Takes on a finite or countable set of values. Described by Probability Mass Function (PMF) $p(x) = P{X = x}$.
- **Continuous RV**: Takes on a continuum of values. Described by Probability Density Function (PDF) $f(x)$ where $P{a \leq X \leq b} = \int\_a^b f(x) dx$.
- **Distribution Function (CDF)**: $F(x) = P{X \leq x}$.
- **Mean (Expectation)**: $E\[X] = \sum x p(x)$ or $\int x f(x) dx$.
- **Variance**: $Var\[X] = E\[X^2] - (E\[X])^2$.

## Review: Conditional Probability and Expectation

- **Conditional Probability**: $P{A|B} = \frac{P{A \cap B}}{P{B}}$ for $P{B} > 0$.
- **Law of Total Probability**: $P{A} = \sum\_i P{A|B\_i}P{B\_i}$ for a partition ${B\_i}$.
- **Conditional Expectation**: Discrete: $E\[X|Y=y] = \sum x P{X=x|Y=y}$. Continuous: $E\[X|Y=y] = \int x f\_{X|Y}(x|y) dx$.
- **Law of Iterative Expectations**: $E\[X] = E\[E\[X|Y]]$.

## Definition: Classification of Stochastic Processes

A stochastic process ${X(t), t \in T}$ is classified by its **state space** ($S$) and its **index set** ($T$):

1. **Discrete-time, Discrete state space**: $T={0,1,2,...}$, $S={0,1,2,...}$. Example: [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov Chain]].
2. **Continuous-time, Discrete state space**: $T=\[0, \infty)$, $S={0,1,2,...}$. Example: Poisson Process.
3. **Discrete-time, Continuous state space**: $T={0,1,2,...}$, $S = \mathbb{R}$.
4. **Continuous-time, Continuous state space**: $T=\[0, \infty)$, $S = \mathbb{R}$. Example: Brownian Motion.

## Definition: Markov property

> Let ${ X\_{t} }$ : [[#Definition Stochastic process|Stochastic process]]
>
> **Markov Property** is the property that, given the value of $X\_{t}$ the values of $X\_{s}$ for $s>t$ are not influenced by the values of $X\_{u}$, $u\<t$

## Definition: Process

## Definition: Discrete-time Markov chain

> Random variable at index n+1 is only dependent with the random variable at n
>
> A [[#Definition: State|state]] depends on the previous "[[#Definition: State|state]]", and only on that previous [[#Definition: State|state]]

$$ P{X\_{n+1} = j | X\_n = i, X\_{n-1} = i\_{n-1}, \dots, X\_1 = i\_1, X\_0 = i\_0} = P\_{ij} $$

> [!tip]
> [[#Definition: State|state]] n+1 is depedent on n, and only n.

## Definition: Transition probability

> Suppose that the (conditional) probability of $X\_{n+1}$ being in state $j$ given that $X\_n$ is in state $i$ is constant over time, i.e.,
> $$ P\_{ij} = P(X\_{n+1} = j \mid X\_n = i), \quad \text{for all } n. $$
> Then $P\_{ij}$ is called the **one-step transition probability**

> [!tip]
> Probability of transitioning from [[#Definition: State|state]] i to [[#Definition: State|state]] j in one time step

## Definition: Transition probability matrix (TPM)

> Full set of all $P\_{ij}$ in matrix form.
>
> $P\_{ij}$ is [[#Definition: Transition probability|transition probability]] from [[#Definition: State|state]] i to j

Often abbreviated to **TPM**

$$
\mathbf{P} = \begin{Vmatrix}
P\_{00} & P\_{01} & P\_{02} & \cdots \\
P\_{10} & P\_{11} & P\_{12} & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \\
P\_{i0} & P\_{i1} & P\_{i2} & \cdots \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots &
\end{Vmatrix}
$$

- [[#Transition matrix example]]

Interpretation:

- $P\_{12}$ is probability of transitioning from [[#Definition: State|state]] 1 to [[#Definition: State|state]] 2 in one step ([[#Definition: Transition probability|Transition probability]])
- $P\_{34}$ [[#Definition: Transition probability|transition probability]] from [[#Definition: State|state]] 3 to 4
- $P\_{55}$ [[#Definition: Transition probability|transition probability]] from [[#Definition: State|state]] 5 to 5

## Procedure: Proving a Valid TPM

1. All entries $P\_{ij} \geq 0$
2. Each row sums to 1: $\sum\_j P\_{ij} = 1$ for all $i$

## Definition: Chapman-Kolmogorov

> [[#Definition: Transition probability|Transition probability]] to go from [[#Definition: State|state]] i to j in n + m steps.

$$P\_{ij}^{(n+m)} = \sum\_{k=0}^{N} P\_{ik}^{(n)} P\_{kj}^{(m)}$$

where $N$ is number of all states

See [[#Proof Chapman-Kolmogorov Equation|proof]]

We say n + m steps to mathematically say we can decompose _any_ multi-step transition into two parts.

We can then "recurse" it by breaking the n steps to k + l step, and so forth.

**Example:**

- 5-step transition from rain to rain =
  - (2 steps to rain, then 3 steps rain→rain) +
  - (2 steps to sunny, then 3 steps sunny→rain)
- 7-step transition:
  - 1-step + 6-step
  - 2-step + 5-step
  - 3-step + 4-step
  - Whatever decomposition is convenient

The equation just formalizes: "to get somewhere in multiple steps, you must pass through some [[#Definition: State|intermediate state]]."

## Definition: n-step Transition Matrix

> Probability that [[#Definition: State|state]] i goes to j in n + m steps

By [[#Definition: Chapman-Kolmogorov|Chapman-Kolmogorov]] equation:
$$
\mathbf{P}^{(n+m)} = \mathbf{P}^{(n)} \mathbf{P}^{(m)}
$$

Also $\mathbf{P}^{(n)} = \mathbf{P}^{n}$ (see [[#Proof n-step Transition Matrix Identity|proof]])

## Proof: n-step Transition Matrix Identity

**Theorem**: For any $n \geq 1$, the n-step Transition Matrix $\mathbf{P}^{(n)}$ is the $n$-th power of the [[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|Transition matrix]] $\mathbf{P}$, i.e., $\mathbf{P}^{(n)} = \mathbf{P}^n$.

**Proof by Induction**:

1. **Base Case ($n=1$)**:
   By definition of [[#Definition: Transition probability|Transition probability]], $P\_{ij}^{(1)}$ is the probability of transitioning from [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$ to $j$ in one step, which is $P\_{ij}$. Thus, $\mathbf{P}^{(1)} = \mathbf{P}^1$.

2. **Inductive Hypothesis**:
   Assume $\mathbf{P}^{(n)} = \mathbf{P}^n$ holds for some $n \geq 1$.

3. **Inductive Step**:
   Consider $\mathbf{P}^{(n+1)}$. By the [[#Definition: Chapman-Kolmogorov|Chapman-Kolmogorov]] equation with $m=1$:
   $$ P\_{ij}^{(n+1)} = \sum\_k P\_{ik}^{(n)} P\_{kj}^{(1)} $$
   Substituting $P\_{kj}^{(1)} = P\_{kj}$ and using the inductive hypothesis $P\_{ik}^{(n)} = (\mathbf{P}^n)_{ik}$:
   $$ P_{ij}^{(n+1)} = \sum\_k (\mathbf{P}^n)_{ik} P_{kj} $$
   The right-hand side corresponds to the entry in row $i$ and column $j$ of the matrix product $\mathbf{P}^n \cdot \mathbf{P}$.
   Therefore, $\mathbf{P}^{(n+1)} = \mathbf{P}^n \cdot \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{P}^{n+1}$.

By induction, the identity holds for all $n \geq 1$.

## Example: Transition matrix

Consider [[#Definition: State|state space]] whether it _rains_ today ([[#Definition: State|state]] 0) or will it be _sunny_ ([[#Definition: State|state]] 1).

Let the [[#Definition: Transition probability|transition probabilities]]:

- $P\_{00}=\alpha$: Probability it _rains_ (0) today, then stay in _rains_ (0) tomorrow
- $P\_{10} = \beta$: Probability It's _sunny_ (1) today, then change to _rains_ (0) tomorrow

The [[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|transition matrix]] is
$$
\mathbf{P} = \begin{Vmatrix} \alpha & 1 - \alpha \ \beta & 1 - \beta \end{Vmatrix}
$$

Where

- $P\_{01} = 1-\alpha$: Probability it _rains_ (0) today, then change to _sunny_ (1) tomorrow
- $P\_{11} = 1-\beta$: Probability it's _sunny_ (1) today, then stay _sunny_ tomorrow

## Example:  n-step transition matrix

Following from [[#Example Transition matrix]], let
$$
\mathbf{P} = \begin{Vmatrix} 0.7 & 0.3 \ 0.4 & 0.6 \end{Vmatrix}
$$

Using [[#Definition: Chapman-Kolmogorov|Chapman-Kolmogorov]] equation, we can compute the probability that if today is rains, then 4 days later it will be rains ($\mathbf{P}^{(4)}\_{00}$) as:

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{P}^{(4)} & = \mathbf{P}^4 \\
& = \begin{Vmatrix}
0.5749 & 0.4251 \\
0.5668 & 0.4332
\end{Vmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

Thus, $\mathbf{P}^{(4)}\_{00} = 0.5749$

And,

- $P^{(4)}\_{00} = 0.5749$: If it rains today, probability it rains 4 days later is 57.49%
- $P^{(4)}\_{01} = 0.4251$: If it rains today, probability it's sunny 4 days later is 42.51%
- $P^{(4)}\_{10} = 0.5668$: If it's sunny today, probability it rains 4 days later is 56.68%
- $P^{(4)}\_{11} = 0.4332$: If it's sunny today, probability it's sunny 4 days later is 43.32%

Note: Each row sums to 1 (total probability from any starting state).

## Examples: More complex n-step transition matrix

**Urn Ball Replacement - Step-by-Step Illustration**

**Setup:**

- Urn always has exactly 2 balls (red or blue)
- At each step: pick a ball, replace it with probability 0.8 same color, 0.2 opposite color
- Initial: both balls red ([[#Definition: State|state]] 2)
- Find: P(5th ball selected is red)

**[[#Definition: State|State Space]]:**

- [[#Definition: State|State]] 0: 0 red balls (both blue)
- [[#Definition: State|State]] 1: 1 red ball, 1 blue ball
- [[#Definition: State|State]] 2: 2 red balls (both red)

**Understanding Transitions from [[#Definition: State|State]] 2 (both red):**

You pick a ball. Since both are red, you definitely pick red.

- With prob 0.8: replace with red → stay at 2 red balls (P\_{22} = 0.8)
- With prob 0.2: replace with blue → now have 1 red, 1 blue (P\_{21} = 0.2)
- P\_{20} = 0 (impossible to go from 2 red to 0 red in one step)

**Understanding Transitions from [[#Definition: State|State]] 1 (1 red, 1 blue):**

You pick a ball. Equal chance of picking red or blue (each prob 0.5).

_Case 1: Pick red (prob 0.5)_

- Replace with red (prob 0.8) → still 1R, 1B ([[#Definition: State|state]] 1)
- Replace with blue (prob 0.2) → now 0R, 2B ([[#Definition: State|state]] 0)

_Case 2: Pick blue (prob 0.5)_

- Replace with blue (prob 0.8) → still 1R, 1B ([[#Definition: State|state]] 1)
- Replace with red (prob 0.2) → now 2R, 0B ([[#Definition: State|state]] 2)

So:

- P\_{10} = 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1
- P\_{11} = 0.5 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.8 = 0.8
- P\_{12} = 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.1

**Understanding Transitions from [[#Definition: State|State]] 0 (both blue):**

Symmetric to [[#Definition: State|state]] 2:

- P\_{00} = 0.8 (pick blue, replace with blue)
- P\_{01} = 0.2 (pick blue, replace with red)
- P\_{02} = 0

**[[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|Transition Matrix]]:** $$ \mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.2 & 0 \ 0.1 & 0.8 & 0.1 \ 0 & 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

**Finding P(5th ball is red):**

The 5th ball is red if at the moment of the 5th selection, we pick a red ball.

Starting at [[#Definition: State|state]] 2 (both red initially), after 4 replacements we need the distribution over [[#Definition: State|states]], then calculate probability of picking red from each [[#Definition: State|state]].

Compute P^(4) starting from [[#Definition: State|state]] 2, then:

- From [[#Definition: State|state]] 0: prob of picking red = 0
- From [[#Definition: State|state]] 1: prob of picking red = 0.5
- From [[#Definition: State|state]] 2: prob of picking red = 1

P(5th ball red) = P^(4)_{20} × 0 + P^(4)_{21} × 0.5 + P^(4)\_{22} × 1

**Computing P^(4) using [[#Definition: Chapman-Kolmogorov|Chapman-Kolmogorov]]**

We'll compute P^(4) = P^(2) × P^(2), breaking the 4 steps into two 2-step segments.

**Step 1: Compute P^(2) = P × P**

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{P}^{(2)} & = \begin{bmatrix}
0.8 & 0.2 & 0 \\
0.1 & 0.8 & 0.1 \\
0 & 0.2 & 0.8
\end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix}
0.8 & 0.2 & 0 \\
0.1 & 0.8 & 0.1 \\
0 & 0.2 & 0.8
\end{bmatrix}\\
& =  \begin{bmatrix}
0.66 & 0.32 & 0.02 \\
0.16 & 0.68 & 0.16 \\
0.02 & 0.32 & 0.66
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

**Step 2: Compute P^(4) = P^(2) × P^(2)**

$$
\begin{align}
\mathbf{P}^{(4)} & = \begin{bmatrix}
0.66 & 0.32 & 0.02 \\
0.16 & 0.68 & 0.16 \\
0.02 & 0.32 & 0.66
\end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix}
0.66 & 0.32 & 0.02 \\
0.16 & 0.68 & 0.16 \\
0.02 & 0.32 & 0.66
\end{bmatrix} \\
& =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.4872 & 0.4352 & 0.0776 \\
0.2176 & 0.5648 & 0.2176 \\
0.0776 & 0.4352 & 0.4872
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

**Final Answer:**

$$
\begin{align}
P(\text{5th ball is red | start with 2 red}) & = P^{(4)}_{20}(0) + P^{(4)}_{21}(0.5) + P^{(4)}\_{22}(1) \\
& = 0.0776(0) + 0.4352(0.5) + 0.4872(1) \\
& = 0 + 0.2176 + 0.4872 \\
& = 0.7048\quad (\text{or } 70.48%)
\end{align}
$$

## Definition: Random Walk Markov Chain

A **Random Walk** is a [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]] whose state space is given by the integers $i = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$ where at each point of time the process either moves:

- One step to the right with probability $p$
- One step to the left with probability $1 - p = q$

$$ P\_{i,i+1} = p, \quad P\_{i,i-1} = 1 - p $$
where $0 < p < 1$.

## Examples: Random walk model

A [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]] whose [[#Definition: State|state space]] is given by the integers i = 0, ±1, ±2, . . . is said to be a random walk if, for some number $0 < p < 1$,

$$
P\_{i,i+1} = p = 1 - P\_{i,i-1}, \quad i = 0, \pm 1, \dots
$$
The preceding [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]] is called a random walk for we may think of it as being a model for an individual walking on a straight line who at each point of time either takes one step to the right with probability p or one step to the left with probability $1 – p$.

## Examples: Gambling model

Consider a gambler who, at each play of the game, either wins $1 with probability $p$ or loses $1 with probability $1-p$. If we suppose that our gambler quits playing either when he goes broke or he attains a fortune of $N$, then the gambler's fortune is a [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]] having [[#Definition: Transition probability|transition probabilities]]

$$
\begin{align}
P\_{i,i+1} & = p = 1 - P\_{i,i-1}, \quad i = 1,2,\dots,N-1, \\
P\_{00} & = P\_{NN} = 1
\end{align}
$$

[[#Definition: Absorbing state|States]] 0 and $N$ are called _[[#Definition: Absorbing state|absorbing]]_ states since once entered they are never left. Note that the preceding is a finite [[#Definition: State|state]] random walk with [[#Definition: Absorbing state|absorbing]] barriers ([[#Definition: Absorbing state|states]] 0 and $N$).

## Proof: Chapman-Kolmogorov Equation

$$
\begin{aligned}
P\_{ij}^{n+m} &= P{X\_{n+m} = j | X\_0 = i} \\
&= \sum\_{k=0}^{\infty} P{X\_{n+m} = j, X\_n = k | X\_0 = i} \\
&= \sum\_{k=0}^{\infty} P{X\_{n+m} = j | X\_n = k, X\_0 = i} P{X\_n = k | X\_0 = i} \\
&= \sum\_{k=0}^{\infty} P\_{kj}^m P\_{ik}^n
\end{aligned}
$$

## Definition: Accessible State

> [[#Definition: State|State]] $j$ is said to be **accessible** from [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$ if $P\_{ij}^n > 0$ for some $n \geq 0$.

This means that starting from [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$, it is <u>_possible_ to eventually enter [[#Definition: State|state]] $j$</u>.

If $j$ is not accessible from $i$, then:
$$
P{\text{ever enter } j \mid \text{start in } i} = \sum\_{n=0}^{\infty} P\_{ij}^n = 0
$$

## Definition: Communication

> Two [[#Definition: State|states]] $i$ and $j$ **communicate** if they are mutually [[#Definition: Accessible State|accessible]]:

- $i \to j$ ([[#Definition: Accessible State|accessible]])
- $j \to i$ ([[#Definition: Accessible State|accessible]])

Notation: $i \leftrightarrow j$

Properties (equivalence relation):

1. **Reflexive**: $i \leftrightarrow i$ (since $P\_{ii}^0 = 1$)
2. **Symmetric**: If $i \leftrightarrow j$, then $j \leftrightarrow i$
3. **Transitive**: If $i \leftrightarrow j$ and $j \leftrightarrow k$, then $i \leftrightarrow k$

_Proof of transitivity_: If $P\_{ij}^n > 0$ and $P\_{jk}^m > 0$, then by [[#Definition: Chapman-Kolmogorov|Chapman-Kolmogorov]]:
$$
P\_{ik}^{n+m} = \sum\_r P\_{ir}^n P\_{rk}^m \geq P\_{ij}^n P\_{jk}^m > 0
$$

## Definition: Communicating Classes

[[#Definition: State|States]] that communicate form a **class**. Classes are either:

- **Identical** (same class), or
- **Disjoint** (no overlap)

## Definition: Irreducible

> The [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]] is **irreducible** if there is only one [[#Definition Communicating Classes|communicating class]] (all [[#Definition: State|states]] are [[#Definition Communication|communicative]]).

## Definition: Recurrent vs Transient

> Transient: Will eventually leave and never come back
>
> Recurrent: Will always come back, no matter how many times it leaves
>
> Notice that unlike [[#Definition Transition probability|transition probability]], this talks about whether or not state will return back to itself in the future.

Let $f\_i$ = probability that, starting from [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$, the [[#Definition: Process|process]] will ever return to [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$.

- **Recurrent**: $f\_i = 1$ (will return with probability 1)
- **Transient**: $f\_i < 1$ (positive probability of never returning)

**Key properties**:

- If $i$ is recurrent: returns infinitely many times
- If $i$ is transient: number of visits to $i$ has geometric distribution with finite mean $1/(1 - f\_i)$
- [[#Definition: State|State]] $i$ is recurrent iff expected number of time periods in [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$ is infinite

## Definition: Period, Periodic, Aperiodic

> the GCD of all steps $n$ at which it is possible to return to state $i$.

The **period** of [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$ is defined as:

$$d(i) = \gcd{n \geq 1 : P\_{ii}^{(n)} > 0}$$

- **Periodic**: period $d > 1$ — returns are confined to multiples of $d$
- **Aperiodic**: period $d = 1$ — no such rhythm, can return at irregular times

## About aperiodic vs periodic state

Everyday illustrations of **Periodic** and **Aperiodic** states:

### Periodic State

A [[#Definition State|state]] $i$ has [[#Definition Period, Periodic, Aperiodic|period]] $d > 1$ if you can only return to that state in a number of steps ($n$) that is a multiple of $d$ (i.e., $n$ is divisible by $d$).

**Everyday Example: A Pendulum or a Swing**

Imagine a child on a swing. Let’s define the **State** as "The swing is at the highest point on the right side."

- If the swing is at the highest point now (Step 0), it must swing down to the middle, then to the left, then back to the middle, before returning to the right.
- It takes exactly **2 full "ticks"** of the movement to return to that specific spot.
- You can only be in that state at Step 2, Step 4, Step 6, etc.
- Because you can **never** be in that state at Step 1, 3, or 5, the state is **Periodic** with a period of $d=2$.

### Aperiodic State

A state is aperiodic if its period $d = 1$. This means you can return to the state at irregular times, and there is no fixed "rhythm" or multiple you must follow.

**Everyday Example: Checking Your Phone for Notifications**

Imagine the **State** is "You are looking at your phone screen."

- You look at your phone now (Step 0).
- You might look at it again **1 minute later** ($n=1$).
- Or you might wait and look at it **5 minutes later** ($n=5$).
- Or **12 minutes later** ($n=12$).
- Because it is possible to return to the state in just 1 step ($n=1$), the greatest common divisor of all possible return times is 1.
- There is no "rule" saying you can only check every 3 minutes; you can return to this state whenever you like. Therefore, it is **Aperiodic**.

## Example: Transient state

When an [[#Definition Absorbing state|absorbing state]] occurs, it's absorbed there forever and never goes to other states, which makes the other states "transient".

Note that the [[#Definition Recurrent vs Transient|transient]] definition only talks about the probability of a state returning to itself, without mentioning about "absorbing". absorbing states are just one scenario that produces it.

Other example is if states ${  2,3,5,6,7}$ and ${ 1,4,8 }$ are two disjoint [[#Definition Communicating Classes|communicating classes]], neither is necessarily transient just from that alone.

Transience depends on whether you can **escape** the class. If from class ${ 1,4,8 }$ there is a positive probability of transitioning into ${ 2,3,5,6,7 }$ but not vice versa, then ${  1,4,8}$ is transient — because once you leave, you never return.

## Definition: Limiting Probability

> For a [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]] ${X\_n, n \geq 0}$, the **limiting probability** $\pi\_j$ of [[#Definition: State|state]] $j$ represents the long-run probability that the [[#Definition: Process|process]] will be in [[#Definition: State|state]] $j$, independent of the initial [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$.
> $$ \pi\_j = \lim\_{n \to \infty} P\_{ij}^n $$
>
> where $P\_{ij}^n$ is the [[#Definition: n-step Transition Matrix|n-step transition probability]] from [[#Definition: State|state]] $i$ to [[#Definition: State|state]] $j$.

The limiting probability is related to the **mean recurrence time** $\mu\_{jj}$ (expected time to return to state $j$ starting from $j$) by:
$$ \pi\_j = \frac{1}{\mu\_{jj}} $$

## Definition: Regular Transition Probability Matrix

> A [[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|TPM]] $\mathbf{P}$ is **regular** if:
>
> 1. For every pair $i, j$, there is a path $k\_1, k\_2, \dots, k\_r$ for which $P\_{i k\_1} P\_{k\_1 k\_2} \dots P\_{k\_r j} > 0$;
> 2. There is at least one state $i$ for which $P\_{ii} > 0$.
>
> Equivalently, $P^k$ has **all positive entries** for some $k > 0$.

Properties:

- If $P^k$ has no zero elements for some $k$, then $P^{k+n}$ will also have no zero elements for all $n \geq 1$
- A regular chain is necessarily [[#Definition Communicating Classes|irreducible]] and [[#Definition Period, Periodic, Aperiodic|aperiodic]] (see [[#Proof Regular chain is necessarily irreducible and aperiodic|proof]])

## Theorem: Limiting Distribution Theorem

> Let $\mathbf{P}$ : [[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|Transition probability matrix (TPM)]] on finite [[#Definition: State|state]] space
>
> Then
> $$
> \begin{gather}
> \exists \pi=(\pi\_{0},\pi\_{1},\dots,\pi\_{N}) \\
> \ni \\
> \pi\_{j} = \lim\_{ n \to \infty }  P\_{ij}^{(n)},\quad\forall i,j
> \end{gather}
> $$
>
> The limiting distribution satisfies:
> $$ \pi = \pi \mathbf{P} \quad \text{and} \quad \sum\_j \pi\_j = 1 $$

## Definition: Stationary Distribution

A probability vector $\pi = (\pi\_0, \pi\_1, \dots)$ is a **stationary distribution** if it satisfies:
$$ \pi = \pi \mathbf{P} \quad \text{and} \quad \sum\_j \pi\_j = 1 $$
If the initial state $X\_0$ is chosen according to $\pi$, then the probability of being in state $j$ at any time $n$ is also $\pi\_j$ (i.e., $P(X\_n = j) = \pi\_j$ for all $n$).

## Definition: Ergodic State

A state is **ergodic** if it is both **positive recurrent** (expected return time is finite) and **aperiodic** (period $d=1$).

- In a finite irreducible Markov chain, all states are positive recurrent.
- If the chain is also aperiodic, all states are ergodic.
- For an irreducible ergodic Markov chain, the limiting distribution always exists and is equal to the unique stationary distribution.
- The limiting distribution, when it exists, is always a stationary distribution. However, a stationary distribution may exist without a limiting distribution (e.g., a periodic chain can have a stationary distribution but no limiting distribution).

## Example: Social Class Problem

Given [[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|TPM]]:
$$
\mathbf{P} = \begin{bmatrix}
\frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\
\frac{1}{4} & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{4} \\
0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Solving $\pi = \pi \mathbf{P}$:
$$
\begin{align}

\pi & = \pi \mathbf{P} \\
\begin{bmatrix} \pi\_{1} & \pi\_{2} & \pi\_{3} \end{bmatrix} & =
\begin{bmatrix} \pi\_{1} & \pi\_{2} & \pi\_{3} \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} & 0 \\
\frac{1}{4} & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{4} \\
0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{1}{2} \\
\end{bmatrix} \\
& = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2}\pi\_1 + \frac{1}{4}\pi\_2 + 0 & \frac{1}{2}\pi\_1 + \frac{1}{2}\pi\_2 + \frac{1}{2}\pi\_3 & 0 + \frac{1}{4}\pi\_2 + \frac{1}{2}\pi\_3 \end{bmatrix}
\end{align}
$$

Matching components:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi\_1 &= \frac{1}{2}\pi\_1 + \frac{1}{4}\pi\_2 \\
\pi\_2 &= \frac{1}{2}\pi\_1 + \frac{1}{2}\pi\_2 + \frac{1}{2}\pi\_3 \\
\pi\_3 &= \frac{1}{4}\pi\_2 + \frac{1}{2}\pi\_3
\end{aligned}
$$

- From equation 1: $\frac{1}{2}\pi\_1 = \frac{1}{4}\pi\_2 \Rightarrow \pi\_2 = 2\pi\_1$
- From equation 3: $\frac{1}{2}\pi\_3 = \frac{1}{4}\pi\_2 \Rightarrow \pi\_3 = \frac{1}{2}\pi\_2 = \pi\_1$
- Substituting into $\pi\_1 + \pi\_2 + \pi\_3 = 1$:

$$
\begin{align}
\pi\_1 + 2\pi\_1 + \pi\_1 & = 1  \\
\quad 4\pi\_1 & = 1  \\
\pi\_1 & = \frac{1}{4}
\end{align}
$$

- Subtituting to $\pi\_{2}$ and $\pi\_{3}$ we obtain:

$$\pi\_2 = \frac{1}{2}, \quad \pi\_3 = \frac{1}{4}$$

## Comparison: Limiting vs. Hitting Probability

Although both often use the notation $\pi$ in lecture slides, they represent fundamentally different concepts:

| Feature          | Limiting Probability $\pi\_j$                                          | Hitting Probability $\pi\_i(j)$                                          |
| :--------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Question**     | "In the long run, what % (proportion) of time is spent in state $j$?" | "Starting at state $i$, what is the chance I **ever** reach state $j$?" |
| **System State** | Describes **steady state** (long-run).                                | Describes the **fate** of a single path.                                |
| **Dependence**   | **Independent** of starting state.                                    | **Heavily depends** on starting state $i$.                              |
| **Matrix Math**  | Left-multiplication: $\pi = \pi \mathbf{P}$                           | Right-multiplication: $u = \mathbf{P}u$                                 |
| **Indices**      | Summing over columns: $\pi\_j = \sum\_k \pi\_k P\_{kj}$                   | Summing over rows: $\pi\_i = \sum\_k P\_{ik} \pi\_k$                        |
| **Boundaries**   | No boundaries; requires $\sum \pi\_j = 1$.                             | **Requires boundaries** (Target=1, Other Absorbing=0).                  |

> [!TIP]
>
> - Use **Limiting Probability** when asked for "Long-run proportion" or "Steady state".
> - Use **Hitting Probability** (via **First Step Analysis**) when asked for "Probability of reaching/absorption" or "Chance of winning".

## Procedure: First Step Analysis

First Step Analysis (FSA) allows you to calculate specific properties of a Markov Chain, such as the probability of reaching a target state or the time until absorption, by analyzing the very first transition.

### Prerequisites

- A defined [[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|Transition Probability Matrix (TPM)]].
- Clear identification of target states (hitting boundaries) and [[#Definition: Absorbing state|absorbing states]].

### Calculating hitting probabilities

Use this procedure to determine the probability that a process starting in state $i$ will eventually hit a specific target state $j$.

1. **Identify boundary conditions.** Set:
   - $\mu\_{\text{target}}=1$
   - $\mu\_{\text{others & absorbing}} = 0$
2. **Formulate equations for transient states.** For each state $i$ that is not a boundary or absorbing state, express the hitting probability $\pi\_i$ as a weighted sum of the probabilities from its possible next states:
   $$\pi\_i = \sum\_k P\_{ik} \cdot \pi\_k$$
3. **Solve the linear system.** Solve the resulting set of equations for all unknown $\pi\_i$ values.

**Outcome:** You will obtain the exact probability of the process ever entering the target state $j$.

### Calculating expected time to absorption

Use this procedure to find the average number of steps required to reach any absorbing state from a starting state $i$.

1. **Set boundary conditions.** For all [[#Definition: Absorbing state|absorbing states]], the expected time to absorption is 0 ($\mu\_{\text{absorbed}} = 0$).
2. **Formulate equations for transient states.** For each transient state $i$, add 1 (representing the current step) to the expected time remaining from all possible next states $k$:
   $$\mu\_i = 1 + \sum\_k P\_{ik} \cdot \mu\_k$$
3. **Solve the linear system.** Solve the resulting equations to find the expected time $\mu\_i$ for each state.

**Outcome:** You will determine the mean number of transitions until the process terminates in an absorbing state.

## Example: First Step Analysis - Gambler's Ruin

Using the scenario from [[#Examples Gambling model]], consider a gambler with $N=3$ and win probability $p=0.4$ (lose probability $q=0.6$).

We want to find the probability of reaching the target ($3$) starting from state $1$.

### Hitting probability

Let $\pi\_i$ be the probability of hitting state $3$ starting from state $i$.

- **Boundary Conditions:** $\pi\_3 = 1$ (Target reached), $\pi\_0 = 0$ (Broke).
- **Equations for Transient States:**
  - $\pi\_1 = 0.4 \pi\_2 + 0.6 \pi\_0 \implies \pi\_1 = 0.4 \pi\_2$
  - $\pi\_2 = 0.4 \pi\_3 + 0.6 \pi\_1 \implies \pi\_2 = 0.4(1) + 0.6 \pi\_1$
- **Solution:**

  $\pi\_1 = 0.4(0.4 + 0.6 \pi\_1) = 0.16 + 0.24 \pi\_1$

  $0.76 \pi\_1 = 0.16 \implies \pi\_1 \approx 0.2105$

### Expected time to absorption

Let $\mu\_i$ be the expected number of steps until reaching state $i$.

- **Boundary Conditions:** $\mu\_0 = 0, \mu\_3 = 0$
- **Equations:**
  - $\mu\_1 = 1 + 0.4 \mu\_2 + 0.6 \mu\_0 \implies \mu\_1 = 1 + 0.4 \mu\_2$
  - $\mu\_2 = 1 + 0.4 \mu\_3 + 0.6 \mu\_1 \implies \mu\_2 = 1 + 0.6 \mu\_1$
- **Solution:**

  $\mu\_1 = 1 + 0.4(1 + 0.6 \mu\_1) = 1 + 0.4 + 0.24 \mu\_1$

  $0.76 \mu\_1 = 1.4 \implies \mu\_1 \approx 1.8421$ steps.

## Proof: Regular chain is necessarily irreducible and aperiodic

> A TPM $\mathbf{P}$ is **regular** if $\mathbf{P}^k$ has **all positive entries** for some $k > 0$.

This means that after $k$ steps, there is a non-zero probability of moving from _any_ state $i$ to _any_ state $j$.

### 1. Proof of Irreducibility

**Definition:** A chain is irreducible if all states communicate ($i \leftrightarrow j$). This means for any two states $i$ and $j$, it is possible to go from $i$ to $j$ and from $j$ to $i$.

**Proof:**

1. By the definition of a regular chain, there exists some $k$ such that $(\mathbf{P}^k)\_{ij} > 0$ for all $i, j$.
2. Since $(\mathbf{P}^k)\_{ij} > 0$, state $j$ is **accessible** from state $i$ ($i \to j$).
3. Similarly, since $(\mathbf{P}^k)\_{ji} > 0$, state $i$ is **accessible** from state $j$ ($j \to i$).
4. Because every state can reach every other state, they all belong to the same **communicating class**.
5. **Conclusion:** The chain is irreducible.

### 2. Proof of Aperiodicity

**Definition:** A state $i$ is aperiodic if its period $d(i) = 1$. The period is the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) of all $n$ such that $(\mathbf{P}^n)\_{ii} > 0$.

**Proof:**

1. In a regular chain, we know $(\mathbf{P}^k)\_{ii} > 0$ for all states $i$. This means $k$ is one possible time the process can return to state $i$.
2. Your notes mention: _"If $\mathbf{P}^k$ has no zero elements... then $\mathbf{P}^{k+n}$ will also have no zero elements for all $n \geq 1$."_
   - _Why?_ Because $\mathbf{P}^{k+1} = \mathbf{P} \cdot \mathbf{P}^k$. To get from $i$ to $j$ in $k+1$ steps, you just need to take one step to _any_ intermediate state $m$, and then take $k$ steps to $j$. Since $\mathbf{P}^k$ is all positive, and you must be able to go _somewhere_ in one step, a path always exists.
3. This implies that if $(\mathbf{P}^k)_{ii} > 0$, then $(\mathbf{P}^{k+1})_{ii} > 0$ as well.
4. The period $d(i)$ must be a common divisor of $k$ and $k+1$.
5. The only positive integer that divides two consecutive integers ($k$ and $k+1$) is **1**.
6. **Conclusion:** $d(i) = 1$ for all states, so the chain is aperiodic.

### Summary

- **Irreducible** because "all positive entries" means you can get **anywhere from anywhere**.
- **Aperiodic** because "all positive entries" means you can return to a state at **any time** $n \ge k$, breaking any fixed "rhythm" or period.

## Definition: Mean Time Spent in Transient States

> For a finite [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]], let $T = {1, 2, \dots, t}$ be the set of **[[#Definition: Recurrent vs Transient|transient states]]**.
> The [[#Definition Transition probability matrix (TPM)|Transition matrix]] restricted to [[#Definition: Recurrent vs Transient|transient states]] is $\mathbf{Q}$ (or $\mathbf{P}\_T$).
>
> The matrix $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ contains the **expected number of time periods** spent in [[#Definition: Recurrent vs Transient|transient state]] $j$ given starting in [[#Definition: Recurrent vs Transient|transient state]] $i$:
> $$ \boldsymbol{\mu} = (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{Q})^{-1} $$
>
> **Properties**:
>
> - $\mu\_{ij}$ (or $\mu\_i(j)$): Expected number of visits to [[#Definition: State|state]] $j$ starting from $i$.
> - $\mathbf{I}$: Identity matrix of size $t$.

## About relation of matrix mu and absorption time

In relation of the mean time matrix $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ ([[#Definition Mean Time Spent in Transient States]]) to the expected time to absorption $\mu\_{i}$ ([[#Expected time to absorption]]):
$$\mu\_{i} = \sum\_{j} \boldsymbol{\mu}\_{ij}$$

Essentially,

- $\mu\_{i}$ is a single value (or vector element) representing total time until any absorption.
- $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is a matrix containing detailed information about visits to _each_ transient state $j$ before absorption.

## Definition: Hitting Probability (Transient)

> The probability that the [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]] ever enters [[#Definition: Recurrent vs Transient|transient state]] $j$ given that it starts in [[#Definition: Recurrent vs Transient|transient state]] $i$ is $\pi\_i(j)$:
> $$
> \pi\_i(j) = \frac{\mu\_{ij} - \delta\_{ij}}{\mu\_{jj}}
> $$
> where $\delta\_{ij} = 1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise.

### Example: Gambler's Ruin (Matrix Analysis)

For $N=7, p=0.4$ (lose prob $0.6$), starting with 3 units:

- $\mu\_{3,5} = 0.9228$ (Expected time at 5 units)
- $\mu\_{3,2} = 2.3677$ (Expected time at 2 units)
- Probability of ever hitting 1: $\pi\_3(1) = \mu\_{3,1} / \mu\_{1,1} = 1.4206 / 1.6149 = 0.8797$

## Definition: Branching Processes

A population model where each individual produces $j$ offspring with probability $P\_j$.

$X\_n$ is the size of the $n$-th generation.

| Moment                   | Formula                                                           |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Mean offspring           | $\mu = \sum\_{j=0}^{\infty} j P\_j$                                 |
| Expected size at gen $n$ | $E\[X\_n] = \mu^n$                                                  |
| Variance at gen $n$      | $\text{Var}(X\_n) = \sigma^2 \mu^{n-1} \dfrac{1 - \mu^n}{1 - \mu}$ |

## Definition: Extinction Probability ($\pi\_0$)

Let $\mu = E\[\xi\_i]$ — the _expected number of offspring per individual_, i.e. the mean of the offspring distribution $P\_j$.

- If $\mu \leq 1$: $\pi\_0 = 1$ (certain extinction, unless $P\_1 = 1$).
- If $\mu > 1$: $\pi\_0$ is the **smallest positive solution** to:

$$\pi\_0 = \sum\_{j=0}^{\infty} \pi\_0^j P\_j$$

Where:

- $\pi\_0$ (left side) — the extinction probability we're solving for
- $j$ — number of offspring a single individual produces
- $P\_j$ — probability of producing exactly $j$ offspring
- $\pi\_0^j$ (right side) — probability that **all** $j$ offspring lines eventually go extinct (each offspring independently has extinction probability $\pi\_0$, and there are $j$ of them)

The right side sums over all possible offspring counts: probability of having $j$ offspring × probability all $j$ lineages die out. This is a self-consistency equation — $\pi\_0$ appears on both sides because extinction of the whole population requires extinction of every sub-lineage.

## Definition: Time Reversible Markov Chains

A stationary Markov chain is time reversible if it satisfies the detailed balance equation

$$\pi\_i P\_{ij} = \pi\_j P\_{ji},\quad\forall i,j$$

> [!tip]
> $\pi\_i$ is the long-run/"overall" proportion/probability of time spent in state $i$. $P\_{ij}$ is the transition probability from $i$ to $j$ in one time step.
>
> $P\_{ij}$ alone = probability of going $i \to j$ given you're already in $i$.
>
> $\pi\_i P\_{ij}$ = probability of going $i \to j$ unconditionally, accounting for how often you're even in $i$ to begin with.
>
> Detailed balance says this frequency is equal in both directions.
>
> <u>Essentially, the rate at which the process goes from $i \to j$ is equal to the rate from $j \to i$.</u>

### Theorem: Kolmogorov's Criterion

A chain is [[#Definition: Time Reversible Markov Chains|time reversible]] iff for any cycle of [[#Definition: State|states]] $i \to i\_1 \to \dots \to i\_k \to i$, the product of [[#Definition: Transition probability|transition probabilities]] is the same in both directions:
$$
P\_{i,i\_1} P\_{i\_1,i\_2} \dots P\_{i\_k,i} = P\_{i,i\_k} P\_{i\_k,i\_{k-1}} \dots P\_{i\_1,i}
$$

### Examples

- **Random Walk on Graphs** (with symmetric weights $w\_{ij} = w\_{ji}$): $P\_{ij} = \frac{w\_{ij}}{\sum w\_{ik}}$ is [[#Definition: Time Reversible Markov Chains|time reversible]].
- **Ehrenfest Urn**: [[#Definition: State|State]] $i$ (number of balls in Urn 1) is [[#Definition: Time Reversible Markov Chains|time reversible]] with $\pi\_i = \binom{M}{i} (\frac{1}{2})^M$.
- **One-closer rule**: Reordering rule that is [[#Definition: Time Reversible Markov Chains|time reversible]], unlike move-to-front.

## Definition: Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

Method to sample from a distribution $\pi$ by constructing a [[#Definition: Time Reversible Markov Chains|time reversible]] [[#Definition Discrete-time Markov chain|Markov chain]].

**Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm**:

1. Propose jump from $i$ to $j$ with $q\_{ij}$.
2. Accept with probability $\alpha\_{ij} = \min \left( 1, \frac{\pi\_j q\_{ji}}{\pi\_i q\_{ij}} \right)$.
