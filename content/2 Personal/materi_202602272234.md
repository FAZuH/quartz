# Week 1: Introduction to Survival Analysis

## Definitions
**Survival Time (T)**: Time until an event occurs; also called time-to-event, death time, or reliability time ^1513f4

**Censored Data**: Incomplete observation data where the event hasn't occurred by the end of study
- Event did not occur until end of study
- Sample unit no longer collectable before end of study

**Truncated Data**: Data with left-truncation (subjects entered study after time 0)

## Concept
Survival analysis studies time-to-event data. Key components:
1. **Origin**: Start of observation time
2. **Scale**: Time measurement unit (days, months, hours)
3. **Event**: The endpoint being measured (death, failure, relapse)

# Week 2: Basic Quantities of Survival Distribution

## Definitions
**Survival Function S(t)**: $S(t) = \Pr(T > t)$ — <u>probability surviving beyond time t</u>

Recall the definition of random variable T [[2 Personal/materi_202602272234#^1513f4\|here]]

**Hazard Function h(t)**: Instantaneous failure rate at time t given survival until t

See [[2 Personal/materi_202602272234#^129274\|conceptual example here]]

**Cumulative Hazard H(t)**: $\int_0^t h(u)du$ — total hazard accumulated up to time t

**Mean Residual Life mrl(t)**: $E[T - t | T > t]$ — <u>expected remaining time</u> after surviving t

**Median Life**: Time where <u>50% of subjects have experienced the event</u>

## Concepts
**S(t) properties**: 
- Monotonically decreasing
- $S(0)=1$, $\lim_{t\to\infty} S(t) = 0$ (starts at 1, approaches 0)

**h(t) properties**: Can be increasing, decreasing, constant, or bathtub-shaped

**Relationship**: $S(t) = \exp[-H(t)]$ and $h(t) = f(t)/S(t)$

**Hazard function**

<u>Rate of the event occurring per unit time (t)</u>, among those who haven't experienced the event yet. ^129274

- A high h(t) at some time t means: among those still alive at t, failures are happening rapidly.
- A low h(t) means failures are rare at that moment.

The "instantaneous" part means you're shrinking the window to a single point in time rather than measuring over a finite interval (like survival function).

100 light bulbs. All running at time t=1000 hours. 5 fail between 1000 and 1001 hours.

$$h(1000) \approx \frac{5/100}{1} = 0.05 \text{ per hour}$$

At t=1000, survivors are failing at a rate of 0.05 per hour.

**Survival vs hazard function**

- Survival describes the subject's survivability for an event (event does not occur)
- Conversely, hazard describes the subject's risk of NOT surviving (event occurs)

**Cumulative hazard**: Accumulated risk over time.

Driving a car: every hour you drive, you face some hazard of an accident. H(t) is the total risk you've accumulated after t hours of driving. Even if the hourly risk is small, H(t) keeps growing the longer you drive.

**Mean residual life**

Try interpreting $E[T-t]$ (given $T>t$) :

- $T-t$ : How much time remains from now ($t$) until the event $(T)$.
- $E[T-t]$ : Mean of time left (survival time)

It's basically the <u>mean of remaining time left before event occurs</u>

## Formulas
$$
\begin{align}
S(t) &= 1 - F(t) \\
h(t) &= \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Pr(t < T \leq t+\Delta t | T > t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)} \\
H(t) &= \int_0^t h(u)du = -\ln S(t) \\
mrl(t) &= E[T - t | T > t] = \frac{\int_t^\infty S(u)du}{S(t)} \\
\mu &= E[T] = \int_0^\infty S(u)du
\end{align}
$$

**Total Time on Test (TTT)**: Cumulative operating time until last failure — sum of all observed times (both event and censored)

$$TTT = \sum_{i=1}^n t_i$$

where $t_i$ is the failure or censoring time for subject $i$.

> [!TIP] Interpretation
> TTT represents the total "machine-hours" or "person-years" that subjects contributed to the study. It's used in:
> - **TTT Plot**: A diagnostic tool to check if data follows Weibull distribution
> - **Nelson-Aalen estimator**: TTT appears in variance calculations

## Hazard function relationship proof


$$
\begin{align}
h(t) & = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \cdot \frac{\Pr(t < T \leq t+\Delta t)}{\Pr(T > t)} \\
 & = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{F(t+\Delta t) - F(t)}{\Delta t \cdot S(t)}
\end{align}
$$

The limit $\lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{F(t+\Delta t) - F(t)}{\Delta t}$ is by definition $F'(t) = f(t)$:

$$\boxed{h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)}}$$

## MRL proof

$$mrl(t) = E[T - t \mid T > t]$$

By definition of [[3 Reference/def-conditional-probability\|Conditional Probability]]. For a [[3 Reference/Def-continuous-random-variable\|Continuous Random Variable]], the conditional density given $T > t$ is:

$$f(u \mid T > t) = \frac{f(u)}{\Pr(T > t)} = \frac{f(u)}{S(t)}, \quad u > t$$

Let $T = u$, then $T - t = u - t$. So, by definition of [[3 Reference/Def-expectation\|Expectation]] for continuous random variable,

$$E[T - t \mid T > t] = \int_t^\infty \underbrace{(u - t)}_{\text{value of }T-t} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{f(u)}{S(t)}}_{\text{pdf of }T}, du$$


[[3 Reference/integration-cheatsheet_202512120549#Integration by Parts\|Integration by parts]] with $v' = f(u)$, $w = u - t$:

$$\int_t^\infty (u-t)f(u),du = \left[-(u-t)S(u)\right]_t^\infty + \int_t^\infty S(u),du$$

The boundary term: at $u=t$, $(t-t)S(t)=0$. At $u=\infty$, $(u-t)S(u)\to 0$ (assuming finite mean). So the boundary term vanishes, leaving:

$$\int_t^\infty (u-t)f(u),du = \int_t^\infty S(u),du$$

Therefore:

$$\boxed{mrl(t) = \frac{\int_t^\infty S(u),du}{S(t)}}$$

# Week 3: Parametric Models

## Exponential Distribution
**Concept**: Simplest model; assumes constant hazard rate. Has "lack of memory" property.

**Formulas** (for $T \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, $\lambda > 0$):
$$
\begin{align}
f(t) &= \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \quad t > 0 \\
S(t) &= e^{-\lambda t} \\
h(t) &= \lambda \quad \\
H(t) &= \lambda t \\
E(T) &= \frac{1}{\lambda}, \quad V(T) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}
\end{align}
$$

Notice that $h(t) = \lambda$. We can interpret $\lambda$ as definition of hazard function:

<u>Rate of the event occurring per unit time (t)</u>, among those who haven't experienced the event yet. 

## Weibull Distribution
**Concept**: Generalization of exponential with shape parameter $\alpha$. Can model increasing, decreasing, or constant hazard.

**Formulas** (for $T \sim \text{Weibull}(\lambda, \alpha)$):
$$
\begin{align}
f(t) &= \alpha\lambda t^{\alpha-1} e^{-\lambda t^\alpha}, \quad t > 0 \\
S(t) &= \exp(-\lambda t^\alpha) \\
h(t) &= \alpha\lambda t^{\alpha-1} \\
H(t) &= \lambda t^\alpha
\end{align}
$$

- $\alpha < 1$: Decreasing hazard
- $\alpha = 1$: Constant hazard (reduces to exponential)
- $\alpha > 1$: Increasing hazard

## Gamma Distribution
**Concept**: Another generalization of exponential.

**Formulas**:
$$f(t) = \frac{\lambda^\alpha t^{\alpha-1} e^{-\lambda t}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}, \quad t > 0$$

## Cheatsheet

||$S(t)$|$h(t)$|$f(t) = S(t)\cdot h(t)$|$H(t)$|$E(T)$|$V(T)$|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|**Exp**|$e^{-\lambda t}$|$\lambda$|$\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$|$\lambda t$|$\frac{1}{\lambda}$|$\frac{1}{\lambda^2}$|
|**Weibull**|$e^{-\lambda t^\alpha}$|$\alpha\lambda t^{\alpha-1}$|$\alpha\lambda t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda t^\alpha}$|$\lambda t^\alpha$|—|—|
|**Gamma**|—|—|$\frac{\lambda^\alpha t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda t}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}$|—|$\frac{\alpha}{\lambda}$|$\frac{\alpha}{\lambda^2}$|

## Formulas
$$
\begin{align}
S(t) &= 1 - F(t) \\
h(t) &= \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Pr(t < T \leq t+\Delta t | T > t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)} \\
H(t) &= \int_0^t h(u)du = -\ln S(t) \\
mrl(t) &= E[T - t | T > t] = \frac{\int_t^\infty S(u)du}{S(t)} \\
\mu &= E[T] = \int_0^\infty S(u)du
\end{align}
$$

**Total Time on Test (TTT)**: Cumulative operating time until last failure — sum of all observed times (both event and censored)

$TTT = \sum_{i=1}^n t_i$$

where $t_i$ is the failure or censoring time for subject $i$.

> [!TIP] Interpretation
> TTT represents the total "machine-hours" or "person-years" that subjects contributed to the study. It's used in:
> - **TTT Plot**: A diagnostic tool to check if data follows Weibull distribution
> - **Nelson-Aalen estimator**: TTT appears in variance calculations

## Example

**Case**: Risk of getting sick during flu season. Hazard increases over time as the season peaks.

Use Weibull with $\lambda = 0.001$, $\alpha = 2$ (increasing hazard).

$$h(t) = \alpha\lambda t^{\alpha-1} = 0.002t$$

|t (days)|h(t)|
|---|---|
|10|0.02|
|30|0.06|
|60|0.12|

**Interpretation of h(t)**: On day 10, survivors (still-healthy people) are getting sick at a rate of 0.02 per day. By day 60, that rate has jumped to 0.12 — the flu season is peaking, risk is much higher.


$$H(t) = \lambda t^\alpha = 0.001t^2$$

|t (days)|H(t)|
|---|---|
|10|0.1|
|30|0.9|
|60|3.6|

**Interpretation of H(t)**: By day 30 you've accumulated 0.9 units of total sickness risk — nearly 1 full unit. By day 60 it's 3.6, and survival probability is $e^{-3.6} \approx 0.027$, meaning only 2.7% of people have avoided getting sick by day 60.

# Week 4: Censoring and Truncation

## Definitions

**Censoring (Penyensoran)**: When the <u>exact survival time is not fully observed</u> — only partial information is available. The subject has not experienced the event by the end of study, or is lost to follow-up before the event occurs.

<u>Mostly about not having enough information.</u>

**Truncation (Pemancungan)**: When subjects are <u>only observed if their event time falls within a certain window</u>. Those whose event occurs outside the observation window are not included in the study at all.

## Concepts

**Types of Censoring**:

1. **Right Censoring**: Event time is <u>beyond</u> a certain point (study ends, subject drops out)
   - Type I: Study ends at fixed <u>time</u> $C$
   - Type II: Study ends when $r$ events <u>occur</u>
   - Random/Progressive: Subject lost to <u>follow-up</u>

1. **Left Censoring**: Event occurred <u>before</u> study started but exact time unknown

2. **Interval Censoring**: Event known to occur within an <u>interval</u> $[L, R]$

| Type              | What we know                  | What we don't know               |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Right censored    | - Survived until time $C$<br> | If/when event occurred after $C$ |
| Left censored     | Event occurred before study   | Exactly when                     |
| Interval censored | Event occurred in $[L, R]$    | Exact time                       |

**Types of Truncation**:

1. **Left Truncation**: Subject <u>enters study after time 0</u>; only observed if $T \geq L$
2. **Right Truncation**: Subject <u>exits study before event</u>; only observed if $T \leq R$

## Formulas

**Likelihood for right-censored data**:
$$
L = \prod_{i=1}^n [f(t_i)]^{\delta_i} [S(t_i)]^{1-\delta_i}
$$

where $\delta_i$ is the event indicator:
- $\delta_i = 1$ if event observed at time $t_i$
- $\delta_i = 0$ if censored at time $t_i$

**Nelson-Aalen & Kaplan-Meier estimators**:
$$
\begin{align}
\hat{H}(t) &= \sum_{t_i \leq t} \frac{d_i}{Y_i} \\
\hat{S}(t) &= \prod_{t_i \leq t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{Y_i}\right)
\end{align}
$$

where:
- $d_i$ = number of events at time $t_i$
- $Y_i$ = number at risk just before $t_i$

## Examples

1. **Clinical Trial**: 30 patients treated for heart disease, observed for 6 years. Only 10 had strokes during the study. The other 20 are <u>right-censored (type I)</u> — we know they survived at least 6 years but don't know when (or if) they will have a stroke.

2. **Carcinogen Study**: 40 mice injected with carcinogen, observed until 25 show disease symptoms. The remaining 15 mice are <u>right-censored (type II)</u> — they may develop disease later but we stopped before observing it.

3. **Survey**: Children asked when they started using gadgets. Some cannot remember exact time (<u>left-censored</u>), some started during the study (observed), some haven't started yet (<u>right-censored (random/progressive)</u>).

---

Key point: Censoring and truncation affect the likelihood function and require special statistical methods (non-parametric like Kaplan-Meier, or semi-parametric like Cox proportional hazards) to properly analyze.

# Week 5: Advanced Censoring & Truncation

## Detailed Censoring (Penyensoran)

Censoring occurs when we have only partial information about the exact survival time.

**Types of Right Censoring (Penyensoran Kanan)**:
1. **Type I (Time Censoring)**: Study ends at a pre-determined time $\tau$ set by the researcher.
   - **Fixed**: All subjects stop at the same time $\tau$ (e.g., mice observed for exactly 14 days).
   - **Progressive**: Different fixed censoring times $C_i$ are assigned at the start.
   - **Generalized**: Subjects enter the study at different times and are censored if the event hasn't occurred when the study ends or they leave.

2. **Type II (Failure Censoring)**: Study ends when a pre-specified number of events $d$ occur among $n$ subjects.
   - **Simple**: Stops exactly at the $d$-th failure. The total duration of the study is a random variable.
   - **Progressive**: Some survivors are intentionally removed from the study at various intermediate event times.

3. **Competing Risk**: Occurs when multiple types of events are possible. The occurrence of one event prevents the observation of others (e.g., in a study on leukemia, death from other causes is a competing risk for disease relapse).

**Left Censoring (Penyensoran Kiri)**: The event occurred *before* a certain time $t_{lc}$, but the exact time is unknown (e.g., a child already knows how to use a gadget before the survey starts).

**Interval Censoring (Penyensoran Interval)**: The event is known to have occurred within a specific interval $[C_l, C_r]$ (e.g., a tumor is detected during a follow-up at 24 months, but was not present at the 18-month check-up).

**Double Censoring (Penyensoran Ganda)**: A dataset that contains both left-censored and right-censored observations (e.g., a gadget usage survey where some children already use them, some start during the study, and some haven't started by the end).

## Truncation (Pemancungan)

Truncation is a **selection mechanism** by design. Only subjects who satisfy certain conditions regarding their survival time $T$ are included in the sample.

1. **Right Truncation (Pemancungan Kanan)**: Only subjects who have **already experienced the event** before a time $T_R$ are included.
   - Condition: $T < T_R$
   - Example: Using historical death records to study lifespan. People who are still alive are not in the records and are excluded from the analysis.

2. **Left Truncation (Pemancungan Kiri / Delayed Entry)**: Only subjects who have **not yet experienced the event** at time $T_L$ are included.
   - Condition: $T > T_L$
   - Subjects must survive until $T_L$ to enter the study (Delayed Entry).
   - Example: 
     - **Nursing Home**: Studying age of death among residents. Subjects must survive long enough to enter the home. Those who die before entering are never observed.
     - **Life Insurance**: Policyholders must be alive at the time they sign up for the policy.

## Comparison Summary

| Feature | Censoring | Truncation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nature** | Missing information about exact time | Selection bias by study design |
| **Awareness** | Researcher knows the subject exists but not the exact $T$ | Researcher may not even know the excluded subjects exist |
| **Likelihood** | Uses $f(t)$ for events, $S(t)$ for censored | Uses conditional probabilities given selection criteria |

## Likelihood Construction Examples

### Right-Censored Data

**Scenario**: 5 patients in a clinical trial. 3 die at times 2, 5, 8; 2 are censored at times 3, 6.

**Step-by-step construction:**
1. For event times ($\delta_i = 1$): contribute $f(t_i)$ to likelihood
2. For censored times ($\delta_i = 0$): contribute $S(t_i)$ to likelihood

$$L = f(2) \cdot f(5) \cdot f(8) \cdot S(3) \cdot S(6)$$

**For exponential model** with constant hazard $\lambda$:
$$L = \lambda e^{-\lambda \cdot 2} \cdot \lambda e^{-\lambda \cdot 5} \cdot \lambda e^{-\lambda \cdot 8} \cdot e^{-\lambda \cdot 3} \cdot e^{-\lambda \cdot 6} = \lambda^3 e^{-\lambda \cdot 24}$$

To find MLE: $\ln L = 3\ln\lambda - 24\lambda$, then $\frac{d}{d\lambda}\ln L = \frac{3}{\lambda} - 24 = 0 \Rightarrow \hat{\lambda} = \frac{3}{24} = 0.125$

### Left-Truncated Data

**Scenario**: Nursing home study. Subject enters at age 70, dies at age 85.

For left-truncated data with entry time $L_i$:
- Must survive until $L_i$ to be observed
- Contribution: $\frac{f(t_i)}{S(L_i)}$ (conditional on surviving until entry)

### Combined Left-Truncated & Right-Censored

For subject who enters at $L_i$, has event/censoring at $t_i$:
$$L_i = \left[\frac{f(t_i)}{S(L_i)}\right]^{\delta_i} \left[\frac{S(t_i)}{S(L_i)}\right]^{1-\delta_i}$$

# Week 6: Non-Parametric Estimation (Penaksiran Non-Parametrik)

## Estimating the Survival Function $S(t)$ (Kaplan-Meier Approach)

The Kaplan-Meier estimator builds the survival curve step-by-step, calculating the conditional probability of surviving past each observed event time.

**Timeline and Definitions:**
- $n = Y_0$: Total number of subjects at the start (time $t_0$). 
- $Y_i$: Number of individuals **at risk** just before time $t_i$.
- $d_i$: Number of **events** (failures/deaths) that occur at time $t_i$.
- $c_i$: Number of **censored** individuals between $t_i$ and $t_{i+1}$.

**Step-by-step Intuition:**
1. **At time $t_0$**: Everyone is alive.
   $S(t_0) = \Pr(T > t_0) = 1$
2. **At time $t_1$**: There are $Y_1$ people at risk. $d_1$ people experience the event.
   The probability of surviving past $t_1$ given survival up to $t_1$ is $1 - \frac{d_1}{Y_1}$.
   $S(t_1) = S(t_0) \times \left(1 - \frac{d_1}{Y_1}\right) = 1 \times \left(1 - \frac{d_1}{Y_1}\right)$
3. **At time $t_2$**: There are $Y_2$ people at risk. Notice that $Y_2$ drops not just because of the deaths $d_1$, but also because of any censored observations $c_1$ that occurred between $t_1$ and $t_2$. So, $Y_2 = n - d_1 - c_1$.
   $d_2$ people experience the event.
   The conditional survival probability is $1 - \frac{d_2}{Y_2}$.
   $S(t_2) = S(t_1) \times \left(1 - \frac{d_2}{Y_2}\right)$

**General Kaplan-Meier Formula:**
$$\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \leq t} \left(1 - \frac{d_i}{Y_i}\right)$$

## Estimating the Cumulative Hazard $H(t)$ (Nelson-Aalen Approach)

The Nelson-Aalen estimator calculates the cumulative hazard by adding up the instantaneous hazard rates at each event time.

**Step-by-step Intuition:**
1. **At time $t_1$**: Out of $Y_1$ people at risk, $d_1$ fail. The hazard rate is $h(t_1) = \frac{d_1}{Y_1}$.
2. **At time $t_2$**: Out of $Y_2$ people at risk, $d_2$ fail. The hazard rate is $h(t_2) = \frac{d_2}{Y_2}$.

**Cumulative Hazard up to $t_2$:**
Simply sum the individual hazards:
$H(t_2) = h(t_1) + h(t_2) = \frac{d_1}{Y_1} + \frac{d_2}{Y_2}$

**General Nelson-Aalen Formula:**
$$\hat{H}(t) = \sum_{t_i \leq t} \frac{d_i}{Y_i}$$

## Variance Estimation: Greenwood's Formula

To estimate uncertainty in $\hat{S}(t)$, use **Greenwood's formula**:

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(t)] = [\hat{S}(t)]^2 \sum_{t_i \leq t} \frac{d_i}{Y_i(Y_i - d_i)}$$

**Intuition**: Kaplan-Meier is a product of conditional probabilities. Using the delta method, the variance of $\ln \hat{S}(t)$ is the sum of variances from each term.

**Simplified form** when $d_i =1$ (no ties):
$$\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(t)] = [\hat{S}(t)]^2 \sum_{t_i \leq t} \frac{1}{Y_i(Y_i - 1)}$$

**For Nelson-Aalen**:
$$\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{H}(t)] = \sum_{t_i \leq t} \frac{d_i}{Y_i^2}$$

---

## Confidence Intervals

### Pointwise CI for $S(t)$

**Linear scale** (can go below 0 or above 1 — not recommended):
$$\hat{S}(t) \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(t)]}$$

**Log-log transform** (recommended — keeps within $[0, 1]$):
$$\left[\hat{S}(t)^{\exp(\mp z_{\alpha/2} \hat{\sigma}(t))}, \hat{S}(t)^{\exp(\pm z_{\alpha/2} \hat{\sigma}(t))}\right]$$

where $\hat{\sigma}^2(t) = \frac{\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(t)]}{[\hat{S}(t) \ln \hat{S}(t)]^2}$

For 95% CI: $z_{0.025} = 1.96$

---

## Summary: Non-Parametric Estimators

| Estimator | Formula | Variance |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| **Kaplan-Meier** $\hat{S}(t)$ | $\prod_{t_i \leq t}\left(1 - \frac{d_i}{Y_i}\right)$ | Greenwood |
| **Nelson-Aalen** $\hat{H}(t)$ | $\sum_{t_i \leq t} \frac{d_i}{Y_i}$ | $\sum \frac{d_i}{Y_i^2}$ |

**Key relationship**: $\hat{S}_{KM}(t) \approx \exp(-\hat{H}_{NA}(t))$

# Week 10: Hypothesis Testing — 1-Sample & k-Sample Tests

## Definitions

**Hypothesis Testing in Survival**: Compare hazard functions across groups. The null hypothesis is that hazard functions are equal.

### 1-Sample Test
Compare sample hazard to a known reference hazard $h_0(t)$.

**Hypotheses**:
$$H_0 : h(t) = h_0(t) \quad \forall t \leq \tau$$
$$H_1 : h(t) \neq h_0(t) \quad \text{for some } t \leq \tau$$

**Test Statistic**:
$$Z(\tau) = O(\tau) - E(\tau) = \sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i) \frac{d_i}{Y(t_i)} - \int_0^\tau W(s) h_0(s) ds$$

With weight $W(t) = Y(t)$ (**1-sample log-rank**):
- $O(\tau) = \sum d_i$ : total observed events
- $E(\tau) = \sum_{j=1}^n [H_0(T_j) - H_0(L_j)]$

Under $H_0$, $\frac{Z(\tau)^2}{\text{Var}[Z(\tau)]} \sim \chi^2_1$.

### k-Sample Test (Log-Rank Test)
Compare hazard functions across $K$ groups.

**Hypotheses**:
$$H_0 : h_1(t) = h_2(t) = \ldots = h_K(t) \quad \forall t \leq \tau$$
$$H_1 : \text{not all equal}$$

**Test Statistic** (group $j$):
$$Z_j(\tau) = \sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)\left[d_{ij} - Y_{ij}\frac{d_i}{Y_i}\right]$$

where:
- $d_{ij}$ = events in group $j$ at $t_i$
- $d_i = \sum_j d_{ij}$ = total events at $t_i$ (pooled)
- $Y_{ij}$ = at risk in group $j$ at $t_i$
- $Y_i = \sum_j Y_{ij}$ = total at risk (pooled)

**Variance** ($\hat{\sigma}_{jj}$) and **Covariance** ($\hat{\sigma}_{jg}$):
$$\hat{\sigma}_{jj} = \sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)^2 \frac{Y_{ij}}{Y_i}\left(1 - \frac{Y_{ij}}{Y_i}\right)\left(\frac{Y_i - d_i}{Y_i - 1}\right)d_i$$
$$\hat{\sigma}_{jg} = -\sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)^2 \frac{Y_{ij}Y_{ig}}{Y_i^2} \left(\frac{Y_i - d_i}{Y_i - 1}\right)d_i$$

**Overall Test**: $\chi^2 = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_{K-1}) \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} (Z_1, \ldots, Z_{K-1})^T \sim \chi^2_{K-1}$

For $K=2$:
$$Z = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)\left[d_{i1} - Y_{i1}\frac{d_i}{Y_i}\right]}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)^2 \frac{Y_{i1}}{Y_i}\left(1 - \frac{Y_{i1}}{Y_i}\right)\left(\frac{Y_i - d_i}{Y_i - 1}\right)d_i}} \sim N(0,1)$$

## Weight Functions

| Test Name | $W(t_i)$ | Characteristic |
|-----------|----------|----------------|
| Log-Rank | $1$ | Equal weight; most powerful under proportional hazards |
| Gehan / Breslow | $Y_i$ | More weight to early times (Wilcoxon generalization) |
| Tarone-Ware | $\sqrt{Y_i}$ | Intermediate |

## R Code: Log-Rank Test

```r
library(survival)

```

# Week 11: Trend & Stratified Tests

## Trend Test
For groups with a **natural ordering** (e.g., disease stage I $\to$ IV).

**Hypotheses**:
$$H_0 : h_1(t) = h_2(t) = \ldots = h_K(t) \quad \forall t \leq \tau$$
$$H_1 : h_1(t) \leq h_2(t) \leq \ldots \leq h_K(t) \quad \forall t \leq \tau$$

with at least one strict inequality. Equivalently:
$$H_1 : S_1(t) \geq S_2(t) \geq \ldots \geq S_K(t)$$

**Test Statistic**: Assign ordered scores $a_1 < a_2 < \ldots < a_K$ (e.g., $a_j = j$):
$$Z = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^K a_j Z_j(\tau)}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^K \sum_{g=1}^K a_j a_g \,\hat{\sigma}_{jg}}} \sim N(0,1)$$

where $Z_j$ and $\hat{\sigma}_{jg}$ are from the k-sample test.

> [!TIP] Interpretation
> A significant trend test means hazard rates increase (or decrease) monotonically across ordered groups. Use this instead of the general k-sample test when groups have a natural ordering — it has higher power for detecting ordered alternatives.

## Stratified Test
Control for **confounding covariates** by testing within homogeneous strata.

**Hypotheses** (for $M$ strata):
$$H_0 : h_{1s}(t) = h_{2s}(t) = \ldots = h_{Ks}(t) \quad \forall s=1,\ldots,M,\; \forall t \leq \tau$$

**Procedure**:
1. For each stratum $s$, compute $Z_{js}(\tau)$ and $\hat{\Sigma}_s$ as in the k-sample test
2. Pool across strata: $Z_{j\cdot}(\tau) = \sum_{s=1}^M Z_{js}(\tau)$ and $\hat{\sigma}_{jg\cdot} = \sum_{s=1}^M \hat{\sigma}_{jgs}$
3. $\chi^2 = (Z_{1\cdot}, \ldots, Z_{K-1\cdot}) \hat{\Sigma}_{\cdot}^{-1} (Z_{1\cdot}, \ldots, Z_{K-1\cdot})^T \sim \chi^2_{K-1}$

## R Code: Stratified Log-Rank Test

```r
# Stratify by logWBC category
dat$category <- cut(dat$logWBC,
                     breaks = c(0, 2.3, 3, Inf),
                     labels = c("low", "medium", "high"))

# Stratified log-rank: treatment effect adjusted for logWBC
fit <- survdiff(Surv(time, status) ~ treatment + strata(category), data = dat)
fit
```

**Example: Larynx Data** — Compare survival across 4 disease stages:
```r

fit  # K-sample log-rank with K=4
```


> [!TIP] Interpretation
> Stratified test answers: "Is there a treatment effect *after* controlling for confounding covariates?" Each stratum is analyzed separately, then results are combined.

## Summary: Hypothesis Testing in Survival

| Test | Statistic | Distribution | When to Use |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| 1-sample | $Z^2/\text{Var}(Z)$ | $\chi^2_1$ | Compare to known $h_0(t)$ |
| k-sample (log-rank) | $\chi^2$ | $\chi^2_{K-1}$ | Compare $K$ independent groups |
| Trend | $Z$ | $N(0,1)$ | Ordered groups (e.g., stage I–IV) |
| Stratified | $\chi^2$ | $\chi^2_{K-1}$ | Control for confounders |

# Week 12: k-Sample Test — Detailed Procedure & Interpretation

## Conceptual Framework

When comparing $K$ groups, the interpretation of "better" depends on the event type:

| Event Type | "Better" Means | Survival Curve | Hazard |
|------------|----------------|----------------|--------|
| **Time-to-relapse** (death, recurrence, failure) | Longer time until event | Higher $S(t)$ | Lower $h(t)$ |
| **Time-to-recovery** (healing, remission, discharge) | Shorter time until event | Lower $S(t)$ | Higher $h(t)$ |

> [!NOTE] Example
> - **Chemotherapy comparison** ($T$ = time to relapse): Better treatment → higher $S(t)$, lower $h(t)$. The curve sits <u>above</u> the control.
> - **Recovery comparison** ($T$ = time to heal): Better treatment → lower $S(t)$, higher $h(t)$. The curve sits <u>below</u> the control.

**Always identify the event direction before interpreting test results.**

## Complete Hypothesis Testing Procedure

### 1. Hypotheses

$$H_0 : h_1(t) = h_2(t) = \ldots = h_K(t) \quad \forall t \leq \tau$$

$$H_1 : \text{at least two hazard functions differ at some } t \leq \tau$$

### 2. Pool Event Times

Combine all $K$ samples. Order the union of distinct event times: $t_1 < t_2 < \ldots < t_D$.

### 3. Construct Contingency at Each $t_i$

|                 | Group 1 | Group 2 | $\cdots$ | Group K | Total |
|-----------------|---------|---------|----------|---------|-------|
| Events ($d$)    | $d_{i1}$ | $d_{i2}$ | $\cdots$ | $d_{iK}$ | $d_i$ |
| At Risk ($Y$)   | $Y_{i1}$ | $Y_{i2}$ | $\cdots$ | $Y_{iK}$ | $Y_i$ |

Under $H_0$, expected events in group $j$: $\displaystyle E[d_{ij}] = Y_{ij} \cdot \frac{d_i}{Y_i}$

### 4. Compute $Z_j$ and $\Sigma$

$$Z_j(\tau) = \sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)\left[d_{ij} - Y_{ij}\frac{d_i}{Y_i}\right]$$

$$\hat{\sigma}_{jj} = \sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)^2 \frac{Y_{ij}}{Y_i}\left(1 - \frac{Y_{ij}}{Y_i}\right)\left(\frac{Y_i - d_i}{Y_i - 1}\right)d_i$$

$$\hat{\sigma}_{jg} = -\sum_{i=1}^D W(t_i)^2 \frac{Y_{ij}Y_{ig}}{Y_i^2} \left(\frac{Y_i - d_i}{Y_i - 1}\right)d_i, \quad g \neq j$$

### 5. Test and Decide

For $K=2$: $\displaystyle Z = \frac{Z_1}{\sqrt{\hat{\sigma}_{11}}} \sim N(0,1)$

For $K>2$: $\chi^2 = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_{K-1}) \hat{\Sigma}^{-1} (Z_1, \ldots, Z_{K-1})^T \sim \chi^2_{K-1}$

**Decision**: Reject $H_0$ if $p\text{-value} < \alpha$.

## R Implementation

```r
library(survival)

# Fit and compare KM curves
fit1 <- survfit(Surv(time1, status1) ~ 1)  # Group 1
fit2 <- survfit(Surv(time2, status2) ~ 1)  # Group 2
# ... fitK for additional groups

plot(fit1, conf.int="none", col="blue", xlab="Time", ylab="Survival")
lines(fit2, conf.int="none", col="red")
# ... lines(fitK, ...)

# Log-rank test
time     <- c(time1, time2)      # pool all times
status   <- c(status1, status2)  # pool all statuses
group    <- c(rep(1, length(time1)), rep(2, length(time2)))
fit      <- survdiff(Surv(time, status) ~ group)
fit
```

**Output interpretation**:
- `Observed`: Actual events per group
- `Expected`: Events expected under $H_0$
- `(O-E)^2/E`: Per-group chi-square contribution
- `Chisq`: Overall $\chi^2$ statistic with $K-1$ df

**Case**: Larynx cancer patients grouped by disease stage (I, II, III, IV). Does survival differ across stages?

# Week 13: Trend & Stratified Tests — Worked Examples

## Trend Test: Larynx Cancer Data

**Goal**: Determine if hazard of death increases monotonically with disease stage (I → II → III → IV).

### Step 1: Visual Inspection

KM curves show Stage I (highest) → Stage IV (lowest), suggesting a monotonic ordering.

### Step 2: Confirm with K-Sample Test

```r
fit <- survdiff(Surv(time, status) ~ as.factor(stage), data = dat)
# Significant → proceed to trend test
```

### Step 3: Trend Test with Scores

Assign scores $a_j = j$ (1, 2, 3, 4). Compute:

$$Z = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^4 a_j Z_j(\tau)}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^4 \sum_{g=1}^4 a_j a_g \hat{\sigma}_{jg}}} \sim N(0,1)$$

In R, using a Cox model with stage as numeric:
```r
fit_trend <- coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ as.numeric(stage), data = dat)
summary(fit_trend)
```

### Step 4: Results

**Coefficients** (stage I as reference):

| Stage | $\hat{\beta}$ | $\exp(\hat{\beta})$ | Interpretation |
|-------|--------------|---------------------|----------------|
| I (ref) | 0 | 1.000 | Baseline |
| II | 0.0648 | 1.067 | 6.7% higher hazard |
| III | 0.6148 | 1.849 | 84.9% higher hazard |
| IV | 1.7349 | 5.667 | 466.7% higher hazard |

**Ordered pattern**: $0 < 0.0648 < 0.6148 < 1.7349$ — confirming increasing trend.

**Likelihood ratio test**: $p = 0.0009$ → reject $H_0$. There is a significant increasing trend in hazard across stages.

### Step 5: Partial Testing — Which Stages Differ?

| Comparison | $p$-value | Significant? |
|------------|----------|--------------|
| Stage II vs I | 0.8876 | **No** — stages I and II are similar |
| Stage III vs I | 0.0949 | Borderline ($\alpha = 0.10$) |
| Stage IV vs I | $< 0.05$ | **Yes** — stage IV clearly differs |

**Conclusion**: Hazard of death increases significantly with larynx cancer stage. The main difference comes from advanced stages (III, IV). Early stages (I, II) are not statistically distinguishable.

> [!TIP] Interpretation
> The trend test captures the **overall pattern** across ordered groups. Partial testing identifies **which specific pairs** drive the difference. Together they provide a complete picture of the group structure.

## Stratified Test: Practical Workflow

### Motivation

Suppose we want to compare survival across $K$ groups while **controlling for a covariate** (e.g., comparing cancer stages while adjusting for sex).

### Procedure

1. **Define strata**: Partition data by covariate (e.g., male, female)
2. **Test within each stratum**: Compute $Z_{js}(\tau)$ and $\hat{\Sigma}_s$ for each stratum $s = 1, \ldots, M$
3. **Pool across strata**: $Z_{j\cdot}(\tau) = \sum_{s=1}^M Z_{js}(\tau)$, $\hat{\sigma}_{jg\cdot} = \sum_{s=1}^M \hat{\sigma}_{jgs}$
4. **Global test**: $\chi^2 = (Z_{1\cdot}, \ldots, Z_{K-1\cdot}) \hat{\Sigma}_{\cdot}^{-1} (Z_{1\cdot}, \ldots, Z_{K-1\cdot})^T \sim \chi^2_{K-1}$

### R Implementation

```r
# Stratified log-rank: compare treatments, adjusted for logWBC
dat$category <- cut(dat$logWBC,
                     breaks = c(0, 2.3, 3, Inf),
                     labels = c("low", "medium", "high"))

fit <- survdiff(Surv(time, status) ~ treatment + strata(category), data = dat)
fit
```

**Example**: Larynx cancer — compare stages while stratifying by sex:

```r
# Each stage's data is partitioned into male/female strata
# Then tested within each stratum, results pooled globally
fit_strat <- survdiff(Surv(time, status) ~ as.factor(stage) + strata(sex), data = dat)
fit_strat
```

> [!TIP] Interpretation
> The stratified test answers: "Do stage effects on survival persist **after controlling for sex**?" A significant result means stage differences are not explained away by sex differences.

| Test | Question | Example |
|------|----------|---------|
| **K-Sample** | Do any groups differ? | Do larynx stages differ in survival? |
| **Trend** | Is there a monotonic pattern? | Does hazard increase with stage? |
| **Stratified** | Is the effect still there after adjusting for covariates? | Does stage matter after controlling for sex? |
| **Partial** | Which specific pairs differ? | Are stages I and II significantly different? |

# Week 14: Semiparametric Model — Cox PH

## Motivation

**Limits of stratification** (covered in Weeks 12-13):
- Covariate categorized as strata requires partitioning data into $K \times M$ subsets → data insufficiency in sparse strata
- Numeric covariates lose information when forced into categories
- Cannot evaluate continuous effects (age, dose) efficiently

**Solution — Regression**: Model hazard as a function of covariates directly, preserving numeric information and handling multiple predictors simultaneously.

## Cox Proportional Hazards Model

### Formula

$$h(t, \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t) \exp(\beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_P x_P) = h_0(t) e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}}$$

In linear form:
$$\log h(t, \mathbf{x}) = \log h_0(t) + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_P x_P$$

### Why Semiparametric?

| Component | Nature | Treatment |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| $h_0(t)$ — baseline hazard | **Non-parametric** | Distribution ignored |
| $e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}}$ — covariate effect | **Parametric** | $\beta_j$ estimated |

$h_0(t)$ is a **nuisance parameter** — inference focuses on $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ only. Since the model has both non-parametric and parametric components, it is **semiparametric**.

### Why "Proportional Hazards"?

For two subjects A and B:

$$\frac{h(t, \mathbf{x}_B)}{h(t, \mathbf{x}_A)} = \frac{h_0(t) e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_B}}{h_0(t) e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_A}} = e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'(\mathbf{x}_B - \mathbf{x}_A)} = \text{constant}$$

The **hazard ratio is constant over time** — it depends only on covariate differences, not on $t$.

## Hazard Ratio (HR)

### Categorical Predictor (Binary $x_1 \in \{0, 1\}$)

$$HR = \frac{h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 \cdot 1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots}}{h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 \cdot 0 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots}} = e^{\beta_1}$$

### Numeric Predictor ($x_2$ increases by 1 unit)

$$HR = \frac{h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2(a+1) + \cdots}}{h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2(a) + \cdots}} = e^{\beta_2}$$

### Interpretation of $\beta$

| $\beta_j$ | $HR = e^{\beta_j}$ | Meaning |
|-----------|-------------------|---------|
| $> 0$ | $> 1$ | Higher $x_j$ → **higher** hazard (risk factor) |
| $= 0$ | $= 1$ | No effect |
| $< 0$ | $< 1$ | Higher $x_j$ → **lower** hazard (protective) |

> [!TIP] Interpretation
> $HR = 2$ means the event rate is twice as high. $HR = 0.5$ means it's halved. The interpretation depends on the event type: for time-to-death, $HR > 1$ is bad; for time-to-recovery, $HR > 1$ is good.

## Dummy Variables for Categorical Predictors

For a variable with $L$ categories, create $L-1$ dummy variables with one category as **reference**.

**Example**: Larynx cancer stage (I–IV). Stage 4 as reference:

$$S_1 = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{stage I} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \quad S_2 = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{stage II} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \quad S_3 = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{stage III} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Model: $h(t, \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 S_1 + \beta_2 S_2 + \beta_3 S_3}$

| Stage | Model | Hazard relative to Stage 4 |
|-------|-------|---------------------------|
| I | $h_0(t) e^{\beta_1}$ | $e^{\beta_1}$ |
| II | $h_0(t) e^{\beta_2}$ | $e^{\beta_2}$ |
| III | $h_0(t) e^{\beta_3}$ | $e^{\beta_3}$ |
| IV (ref) | $h_0(t)$ | 1 |

### Handling Numeric & Mixed Predictors

**Numeric only**: $h(t, \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 \cdot \text{age}}$

**With quadratic term**: $h(t, \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 \cdot \text{age} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{age}^2}$

**Mixed (numeric + categorical)**: $h(t, \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 S_1 + \beta_2 S_2 + \beta_3 S_3 + \beta_4 \cdot \text{age}}$

**With interaction** (stage × age):
$$h(t, \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 S_1 + \beta_2 S_2 + \beta_3 S_3 + \beta_4 \cdot \text{age} + \beta_5 S_1 \cdot \text{age} + \beta_6 S_2 \cdot \text{age} + \beta_7 S_3 \cdot \text{age}}$$

> [!NOTE]
> Interactions increase model complexity. Always test whether the interaction term is significant before including it.

## Checking the PH Assumption

### Log-Cumulative-Hazard Plot

From the Cox PH model: $\ln(-\ln S(t, \mathbf{x})) = \boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x} + \ln(-\ln S_0(t))$

For two groups, the difference $\ln H(t, \mathbf{x}_B) - \ln H(t, \mathbf{x}_A)$ should be **constant over time**. This means the log-cumulative-hazard curves should be **parallel**.

**R implementation**:
```r
library(survival)

# Fit KM by group
fit <- survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ treat, data = leukemia)

# Log-cumulative-hazard plot (fun = "cloglog")
plot(fit, fun = "cloglog", col = c("blue", "red"),
     xlab = "Time", ylab = "log(-log(S(t)))",
     main = "PH Assumption Check")
legend("bottomright", c("6-MP", "Placebo"),
       col = c("blue", "red"), lty = 1)
```

**Decision**:
- **Parallel curves** → PH holds → proceed with Cox PH
- **Intersecting/diverging curves** → PH violated → use stratified Cox or time-dependent covariates

**Example**: Leukemia data (6-MP vs placebo) — the log-cumulative-hazard curves are approximately parallel. The PH assumption is reasonable for the treatment variable.

### Formal Tests

```r
fit_cox <- coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ treat, data = leukemia)
cox.zph(fit_cox)  # Schoenfeld residuals test
```

A significant p-value in `cox.zph` suggests PH violation for that covariate.

See [[3 Reference/cox-proportional-hazards-model_202605051448\|Cox PH Model]] and [[3 Reference/checking-proportional-hazards-assumption_202605051449\|Checking PH Assumption]] for detailed reference.

# Week 15: Cox PH — Likelihood Construction

## Parameter Estimation Overview

Two approaches:

1. **Parametric**: Assume $h_0(t)$ follows a known distribution (e.g., Weibull)
2. **Semiparametric (Cox PH)**: Ignore the distribution of $h_0(t)$, treat it as a nuisance parameter

On the Cox approach, the full likelihood factorizes:
$$L(\boldsymbol{\beta}, h_0(\cdot)) = L_1(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \times L_2(\boldsymbol{\beta}, h_0)$$

$L_1(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ is the **partial likelihood** — inference focuses on $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ only.

## Partial Likelihood (No Ties)

Let $D$ be the number of distinct event times: $t_1 < t_2 < \ldots < t_D$. At each $t_j$, let $R_j$ be the **risk set** — all subjects still at risk just before $t_j$.

$$\boxed{L_1(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{j=1}^D \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_{(j)}}}{\sum_{l \in R_j} e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_l}}}$$

**Intuition**: At each event time $t_j$, the contribution is the ratio of the "victim's" hazard to the sum of hazards of everyone at risk:

$$L(t_j) = \frac{h_0(t_j) e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_{(j)}}}{\sum_{l \in R_j} h_0(t_j) e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_l}} = \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_{(j)}}}{\sum_{l \in R_j} e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_l}}$$

$h_0(t_j)$ cancels — it appears in both numerator and denominator. Multiply across all $D$ event times to get $L_1(\boldsymbol{\beta})$.

## Partial Likelihood (With Ties)

When $d_i > 1$ events occur at the same time $t_i$, three methods handle ties:

### Notation
- $\mathcal{D}_i$ : set of $d_i$ subjects experiencing event at $t_i$
- $\mathbf{Z}_i = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{D}_i} \mathbf{x}_k$ : sum of covariate vectors of event subjects

### Breslow Method

Treats events as sequential but does **not** update the risk set between them.

$$\boxed{L_{\text{Breslow}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^D \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{Z}_i}}{\left[\sum_{l \in R_i} e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_l}\right]^{d_i}}}$$

### Efron Method

Like Breslow, but reduces the denominator by a fraction of the event subjects' hazard at each step.

$$\boxed{L_{\text{Efron}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^D \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{Z}_i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{d_i} \left[\sum_{l \in R_i} e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_l} - \frac{j-1}{d_i}\sum_{k \in \mathcal{D}_i} e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_k}\right]}}$$

### Discrete (Exact) Method

Enumerates all $\binom{Y_i}{d_i}$ possible event combinations and sums their hazards.

$$\boxed{L_{\text{Discrete}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \prod_{i=1}^D \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{Z}_i}}{\sum_{q \in Q_i} e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{Z}_q^*}}}$$

## Example: Burn Data

**Setup**: 154 burn patients. Model: $h(t, \text{gender}) = h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 \cdot \text{gender}}$, with gender = 1 (female), 0 (male).

**At $t = 4$**: $Y = 145$ at risk (31 female, 114 male). $d = 5$ events (1 female, 4 male).

**Breslow contribution**:
$$L_{\text{Breslow}}(t=4) = \frac{e^{\beta_1}}{[114 + 31 e^{\beta_1}]^5}$$

**Efron contribution** — total hazard of 5 event subjects: $4 + e^{\beta_1}$:
$$L_{\text{Efron}}(t=4) = \frac{e^{\beta_1}}{\prod_{j=1}^5 \left[31 e^{\beta_1} + 114 - \frac{j-1}{5}(4 + e^{\beta_1})\right]}$$

**Discrete contribution** — 6 possible gender combinations for 5 events:
$$\sum_{l=0}^5 \binom{114}{l} \binom{31}{5-l} e^{(5-l)\beta_1}$$

## Method Comparison

| Method | Accuracy | Speed | R Default? |
|--------|----------|-------|------------|
| Breslow | Good for few ties | Fastest | No |
| **Efron** | Better | Moderate | **Yes** |
| Discrete | Exact | Slow (combinatorial) | No — use `method = "exact"` |

> [!NOTE]
> R's `coxph()` uses **Efron** by default. Specify `method = "breslow"` or `method = "exact"` to change.

## R: Fitting a Cox PH Model

```r
library(survival)

# Fit Cox PH model
fit <- coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ age + sex + stage, data = dat)

# Results
summary(fit)              # coefficients, HR, CI, p-values
exp(coef(fit))            # hazard ratios
exp(confint(fit))         # 95% CI for HR

# PH assumption check
cox.zph(fit)

# Baseline survival curve
base_surv <- survfit(fit)
plot(base_surv, col = "blue", xlab = "Time", ylab = "Baseline Survival")
```

## Summary: Cox PH Model Construction

| Component | Formula | Example |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| **Model** | $h_0(t) e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}}$ | $h_0(t) e^{\beta_1 \cdot \text{stage} + \beta_2 \cdot \text{age}}$ |
| **HR (categorical)** | $e^{\beta_j}$ | $e^{\beta_1}$ = stage effect vs reference |
| **HR (numeric)** | $e^{\beta_j}$ per unit | $e^{\beta_2}$ = effect per year of age |
| **Partial likelihood** | $\prod \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{x}_{(j)}}}{\sum_{R_j}}$ | No ties |
| **Ties: Breslow** | $\prod \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{Z}_i}}{[\sum_{R_i}]^{d_i}}$ | Approximate |
| **Ties: Efron** | $\prod \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}'\mathbf{Z}_i}}{\prod_{j=1}^{d_i} [\sum_{R_i} - \frac{j-1}{d_i}\sum_{\mathcal{D}_i}]}$ | R default |
| **PH check** | `cox.zph(fit)` or `plot(fit, fun="cloglog")` | Parallel = PH holds |
