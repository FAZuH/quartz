## Lesson 10: [The て form](https://yoku.bi/Section1/Part1/Lesson10.html#the-%E3%81%A6-form)

> There is a verb form that ends in て. This verb form is used in two main ways:
>
> - to chain actions or statements together;
> - to provide additional nuance to an action or statement.

> For verbs, the て form is made by replacing the past tense (from \[\[#Lesson 9 \[Past verbs]\(https //yoku.bi/Section1/Part1/Lesson9.html past-verbs)|lesson 9]]) た with て
> (does not actually encode any "pastness")
>
> The godan verb 行く is an exception and its て form is 行って and **not** 行いて.

> [!example] て-form for verbs
>
> - 殺**して**
> - 切**って**
> - 買**って**
> - 死**んで**
> - 食**べて**

> Adjectives can also have a て form. This is done by turning the い into くて.
>
> The adjective いい is an exception and it becomes よくて.

> [!example] て-form for adjectives
> |         |          |
> | ------- | -------- |
> | 赤**い**  | 赤**くて**  |
> | 怖**い**  | 怖**くて**  |
> | 面白**い** | 面白**くて** |
> | い**い**  | よ**くて**  |

> This also works for negative conjugations, which is also how we apply て form to negative verbs.

> [!example] て-form for negative conjugations
> |          |           |
> | -------- | --------- |
> | 赤くな**い** | 赤くな**くて** |
> | 食べな**い** | 食べな**くて** |

> At the very basic syntax level, AてB means "A happens, and then B"

> [!example]
> 察しがよく**て**助かる
> (lit. you have good intuition **and** that helps me)

## Lesson 9: [Past verbs](https://yoku.bi/Section1/Part1/Lesson9.html#past-verbs)

> You form the past tense of a verb by attaching た or だ to a specific "stem".

> For ichidan, just replace the る with a た.

> [!example] Ichidan past verbs
> |Non-past|Past|
> |---|---|
> |見**る**|見**た**|
> |食べ**る**|食べ**た**|

> For godan,
>
> - う, つ, or る become った
> - す becomes した
> - ぶ, む, or ぬ become んだ
> - く and ぐ become respectively いた and いだ

> [!example] Godan past verbs
>
> |     |     |     |     |     |     |     |     |     |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | 切る  | 殺す  | 死ぬ  | 選ぶ  | 読む  | 買う  | 動く  | 急ぐ  | 持つ  |
> | 切った | 殺した | 死んだ | 選んだ | 読んだ | 買った | 動いた | 急いだ | 持った |

> There are also a couple of verbs with a funny irregular conjugation, for example 問う becomes 問うた, but rare.

## Lesson 8: い-adjectives

> い-adjectives act similar to verbs and can conjugate

> な-adjectives act like (and often can be used as) nouns

> [!example]
> 火が赤い
> The fire is red.
>
> **赤い**火
> Red fire

> [!example] です is a politeness marker when not attached to a noun
>
> 火が赤い
> The fire is red
>
> 火が赤い**です**
> The fire is red (polite)

> Replace い with かった/くない to attach a past/negative conjugation
>
> The adjective いい is an exception and it becomes よい -> よかった / よくない

> [!example] い-adjective conjugations
> 火が赤<u>かった</u>
> The fire **was** red
>
> 火が赤<u>くない</u>
> The fire **is not** red
>
> 火が赤<u>くなかった</u>
> The fire **was not** red
>
> > In the 2nd example, the ない behaves as an い-adjective, so we can conjugate it into なかった just like in the 3rd example

> い-adjectives can be turned into abstract noun, most commonly with さ

> [!example]
> 大きい
> large/big (adj)
>
> 大きさ
> largeness/size (noun)

## Lesson 6: Particles に, へ, and から

> - から :  <u>starting point</u> of an action
> - へ / に : <u>ending point</u> / <u>direction</u> of an action

> The subject can be dropped in Japanese if it's understood or not important

> [!example] から and へ/に
> 学校**から**家**へ**帰る
> return home **from** school
>
> 学校**から**家**に**帰る
> return home **from** school

> に
>
> - in (location of existence)
> - to (target of direction)
> - at (point in time)
> - indirect object/target of an action

> [!example] に
> 部屋**に**いる
> be **in** the room
>
> 友達**に**あげる
> give **to** a friend
>
> 三時**に**会う
> meet **at** 3 o'clock

> へ
>
> - to/toward (most situations), including abstract ones.
> - on

> [!example] へ
> 東京**へ**行く
> go **to** Tokyo

> へ and に can be interchangeable with very minimal difference in meaning.
>
> へ sometimes sound a bit more <u>formal or literary</u>.

> に can mark a location of existence or target of direction. The latter use overlaps with へ a little, but へ cares more about motion and "towardsness", and に cares more about the end objective. It can also mark a point in time when an action takes place.

## Lesson 5: Posession and attributes with no

> <u>の is a particle. It's a possessive marker and an attributive marker.</u>
>
> "Attributive" here means that something is an attribute of something else, like an attribute in a video game, like the "flame" in "flame sword". The relationship is always straightforward.

> [!example] Possession
> 私**の**本
> **my** book
>
> 田中さん**の**車
> Tanaka's car

> It's like "of", but it applies backwards
>
> Sometimes "of" gets confusing, and you have to remember the idea of "attribute".

> [!example] Attribution
> 炎**の**剣
> flame sword / sword **of** flame
>
> 日本語**の**先生
> Japanese teacher / teacher **of** Japanese

## Lesson 4: Ichidan and Godan verbs

> <u>Ichidan verbs always end in る, and just drop the る when they conjugate</u>. The stuff leading up to that る is the verb's basic identity, and isn't changed. You can call it the "stem" of the verb. The verb itself, aside from the conjugation, has only "one form".

> [!example] Ichidan verb
> 食べ**る**
> to eat
>
> 食べ**た**
> ate (past)

> <u>Godan verbs can end in one of several syllables, not just る</u>. In the dictionary form, this syllable always ends in a u-sounding vowel, and different conjugations can change it to any of the other four vowels. This means that it can have any of five different vowels at the end. In other words, "five forms" or stems.

> [!example] Godan verbs
> 書**く** -> 書**か**ない (write -> not write)
> 読**む** -> 読**み**ます (read -> read polite)
> 買**う** -> 買**っ**た (buy -> bought)

## Lesson 3: Particles and grammatical case

> Japanese particles come after the phrase or word they're modifying instead of before it

> <u>Japanese uses the "topic marker" particle は, which literally just says "this is what I'm talking about"</u>

> [!example] Topic marker は
> 私**は**学生だ
> **As for me**, I am a student

> In English, we use word order to understand what role each word has in a sentence: subject, verb, object.
>
> These roles are called "cases", and each role is a specific "case". Japanese uses particles to indicate these roles, that's why they are often called "case-marking particles"

> [!example] Case-marking particles
> 私**が**食べる
> **I** eat (subject marker が)
>
> りんご**を**食べる
> eat **an apple** (object marker を)

## Lesson 2: Nouns, pronouns

> <u>Most of the time, Japanese does not distinguish between singular and plural</u>. Nouns and verbs don't have to agree for plurality, person, or gender

> [!example]
> 本 can mean "book" or "books"
> 猫 can mean "cat" or "cats"

> Japanese can explicitly refer to a group by attaching certain suffixes to nouns, but none of them are a true plural

> Japanese pronouns never change form for cases like English ones do (he vs him)

> [!example]
> 私**は** / 私**が** / 私**を**
> I (subject) / I (subject) / me (object)
>
> 彼**は** / 彼**が** / 彼**を**
> he (subject) / he (subject) / him (object)

## Lesson 1: State of being with だ and です

> A **copula** is a word that links a subject to a predicate (usually a noun or adjective), expressing "is/am/are."

> [!example] Copulas
>
> English: _She **is** a student._
> Japanese: _彼女は学生**だ**。_
>
> だ and です are Japanese copulas — they assert identity or state.

| Politeness | Tense    | Affirmative | Negative                                                     |
| ---------- | -------- | ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Casual** | Non-Past | だ           | <u>じゃ</u> ない<br><u>では</u> ない                                 |
| **Casual** | Past     | だった         | <u>じゃ</u> なかった<br><u>では</u> なかった                             |
| **Polite** | Non-Past | です          | <u>じゃ</u> ないです<br><u>じゃ</u> ありません<br><u>では</u> ありません         |
| **Polite** | Past     | でした         | <u>じゃ</u> なかったです<br><u>じゃ</u> ありませんでした<br><u>では</u> ありませんでした |
**Notes:**

- **じゃ** is a casual slurring of **では**
- **じゃないです** is a common alternative to the longer **～ありません** forms

> Two copulas in Japanese are だ and です. です is more polite than だ

> だ is usually omitted at the end of conversational sentences in modern Japanese.だ

> while です is the polite version of だ, the two aren't always interchangeable and have a lot of different uses

> When it's not attached to a noun, です is just a politeness marker.

> To state that something is not, we turn the だ into ではない and です into ではありません

> In speech the では gets slurred into じゃ so じゃない and じゃありません are more common.

> The past form of だ is だった. The past form of です is でした.

## Lesson 0: The anatomy of Japanese sentences

> <u>Japanese a pro-drop language</u>. What this means is that anything that can be implied or understood from the context can be omitted

> Most Japanese conversations begin by <u>setting the stage of what is being talked about, and then add more details with every new statement that relates back to the topic</u>

> [!example] Pro-drop and topic-prominence
> Sounds like backwards caveman speak
>
> - English: "I ate an apple then drank some water. What did you do?"
> - Japanese: "**About me**, apple ate. Later, water drank. **About you**?"
