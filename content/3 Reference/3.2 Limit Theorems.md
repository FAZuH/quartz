## 3.2.1 Definition: Bounded sequence

> A [[3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.1 Definition Sequence of real numbers|sequence]] $X = (x\_n)$ of real numbers is said to be [[2.3 The Completeness Property of R#2.3.1 Definition Bounded set|bounded]].
>
> if there exists a real number $M > 0$
> such that $|x\_n| \leq M$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.

Thus the **sequence of real numbers $(x\_n)$ is bounded**
if and only if the set of its values are a _bounded subset of $\mathbb R$_.

> [!NOTE]
>
> To prove a sequence is bounded, find $M$ which $\forall n \in \mathbb N$ satisfies $|x\_n| < M$.
>
> We can then prove using $-M \leq x\_n \leq M$.
>
> Do example with $|\frac 1 n|$!

## 3.2.2 Theorem: A converging sequence of real numbers is bounded

> A **[[3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.3 Definition Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence|converging sequence]] of real numbers is bounded**.

## Definition: Algebra operations on sequences

> If $X = (x\_n)$ and $Y = (y\_n)$ a sequence of real numbers, and $c \in \mathbb R$ then:
>
> - $X + Y := (x\_n + y\_n)$
> - $X - Y := (x\_n - y\_n)$
> - $XY := (x\_ny\_n)$
> - $\dfrac X Y := \left(\dfrac{x\_n}{y\_n}\right)$ with $y\_n \neq 0,\ \forall n \in \mathbb N$
> - $cX := (cx\_n)$

## 3.2.3 Theorem: Convergence and algebra operations

> 1. Let $X = (x\_n)$ and $Y = (y\_n)$ be sequences of real numbers that converge to $x$ and $y$, respectively, and let $c \in \mathbb R$.
>
> Then the sequences **$X + Y, X - Y, X \cdot Y$, and $cX$ converge to $x + y, x - y, xy$, and $cx$**, respectively.
> 2\. If $X = (x\_n)$ converges to $x$ and $Z = (z\_n)$ is a sequence of nonzero real numbers that converges to $z$ and if $\lim(z\_n) = z \neq 0$.
>
> Then the quotient sequence **$X/Z$ converges to $x/z$**.

Notice the difference from the previous definition.

In this theorem, $X$ and $Y$ has to be convergent, so that the resulting limit is also convergent.

We use [[3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.10 Theorem Finding limit of a sequence|Theorem 3.1.10]] to prove this theorem. See the example attached to that theorem.

## 3.2.4 Theorem: Limit of positive sequences

> If $X = (x\_n)$ is a _convergent_ sequence of real numbers
> and if _$x\_n \geq 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb N$_,
>
> then **$x = \lim(x\_n) \geq 0$**.

$X$ is converging, and all its values are positive.

The limit must also be positive.

## 3.2.5 Theorem: Order preservation in limits of sequences

> If $X = (x\_n)$ and $Y = (y\_n)$ are _convergent_ sequences of real numbers
> and if _$x\_n \leq y\_n$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$_,
>
> then **$\lim(x\_n) \leq \lim(y\_n)$**.

In essence similar to [[#3.2.4 Theorem Limit of positive sequences|3.2.4]].

The difference is instead of comparing with $0$, we're comparing with another limit.

## 3.2.6 Theorem: Bounds of limit of sequences

> If $X=(x\_n)$ is a _convergent_ sequence
> and if _$a \leq x\_n \leq b$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$_,
>
> then **$a\leq\lim(x\_n)\leq b$**.

Notion of [[2.3 The Completeness Property of R#2.3.1 Definition Bounded set|bounds]] in converging sequences.

Extension of [[#3.2.1 Definition Bounded sequence|3.2.1]] and [[#3.2.2 Theorem A converging sequence of real numbers is bounded|3.2.2]]

## 3.2.7 Theorem: Squeeze theorem

> Suppose that $X=(x\_n), Y=(y\_n)$, and $Z=(z\_n)$ are sequences of real numbers such that
> $$
> x\_n \leq y\_n \leq z\_n, \quad \forall n\in\mathbb N
> $$
> and that _$\lim(x\_n) = \lim(z\_n)$_.
>
> Then **$y = (y\_n)$ is convergent** and
> **$$
> \lim(x\_n) = \lim(y\_n) = \lim(z\_n)
> $$**

## 3.2.9 Theorem: Limit of absolute sequences

> Let the sequence $(x\_n)$ _converge to $x$_.
>
> Then the sequence **$(|x\_n|)$ of absolute values converges to $|x|$**.
> That is, $x = \lim(x\_n) \to |x| = \lim(|x\_n|)$

From [[2.2 Absolute Value and the Real Line#2.2.3 Theorem Triangle inequality|Triangle Inequality]].

## 3.2.10 Theorem: Limit of square root of sequences

> Let the sequence $(x\_n)$ _converge to $x$_.
> and suppose that _$x\_n\geq 0$_.
>
> Then the sequence **$(\sqrt{x\_n})$ of positive square roots converges to $\sqrt{ x }$**

## Proof

### 3.2.2

1. Premise

> Let $(x\_n)$ be sequence of real numbers, and $\varepsilon := 1$
> Suppose that $\lim(x\_n) =x$

2. From premise. Using [[3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.3 Definition Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence|Definition 3.1.3]]

> Then there exists a natural number $K = K(1)$
> such that $|x\_n - x| < 1$ for all $n \geq K$.

3. From 2. Using [[2.2 Absolute Value and the Real Line#2.2.3 Theorem Triangle inequality|Triangle Inequality]]

> If we apply the Triangle Inequality with $n \geq K$, we obtain
> $$
> \textcolor{yellow}{|x\_n|} = |x\_n - x + x| \leq |x\_n - x| + |x| \textcolor{yellow}{< 1 + |x|}
> $$

4. Define.

> If we set
> $$
> \textcolor{lime}M := \sup\set{|x\_1|, |x\_2|, \dots, |x\_{K-1}|, \textcolor{yellow}{1 + |x|}},
> $$

5. From 3, 4.

> then it follows that $\textcolor{yellow}{|x\_n|} \leq \textcolor{lime}M$ for all $n \in \mathbb N$.

> [!NOTE]
> Notice that in $\set{|x\_1|, |x\_2|, \dots, |x\_{K-1}|}$ the subscripts are natural numbers.
>
> Then it follows that the inequality applies for all $n\in\mathbb N$

### 3.2.4

Suppose the conclusion is not true and that $x < 0$;
then $\varepsilon := -x$ is positive.
Since $X$ converges to $x$,
there is $K\in\mathbb N$ such that
$$
x - \varepsilon < x\_n < x + \varepsilon \qquad \forall n \geq K
$$
In particular, we have $x\_K < x + \varepsilon = x + (-x) = 0$.
But this contradicts the hypothesis that $x\_n \geq 0$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.
Therefore, this contradiction implies that $x\geq 0$.

### 3.2.5

Let $z\_n := y\_n - x\_n$ so that $Z := (z\_n) = Y - X$
and $z\_n \geq 0$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.
It follows from Theorems 3.2.3 and 3.2.4 that
$$
0 \leq \lim Z = \lim(y\_n) - \lim(x\_n)
$$
so that $\lim(x\_n) \leq \lim(y\_n)$

### 3.2.6

Let $Y$ be the constant sequence $(b,b,b,\dots)$.
Theorem 3.2.5 implies that $\lim X \leq \lim Y = b$.
Similarly one shows that $a \leq \lim X$.
