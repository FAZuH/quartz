## 3.2.1 Definition: Bounded sequence

> A [[3 Reference/3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.1 Definition Sequence of real numbers\|sequence]] $X = (x_n)$ of real numbers is said to be [[3 Reference/2.3 The Completeness Property of R#2.3.1 Definition Bounded set\|bounded]].
> 
> if there exists a real number $M > 0$
> such that $|x_n| \leq M$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.

Thus the **sequence of real numbers $(x_n)$ is bounded**
if and only if the set of its values are a *bounded subset of $\mathbb R$*.

> [!NOTE]
>	
> To prove a sequence is bounded, find $M$ which $\forall n \in \mathbb N$ satisfies $|x_n| < M$.
>	
> We can then prove using $-M \leq x_n \leq M$.
>	
> Do example with $|\frac 1 n|$!
	

## 3.2.2 Theorem: A converging sequence of real numbers is bounded

> A **[[3 Reference/3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.3 Definition Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence\|converging sequence]] of real numbers is bounded**.

## Definition: Algebra operations on sequences

> If $X = (x_n)$ and $Y = (y_n)$ a sequence of real numbers, and $c \in \mathbb R$ then:
> - $X + Y := (x_n + y_n)$
> - $X - Y := (x_n - y_n)$
> - $XY := (x_ny_n)$
> - $\dfrac X Y := \left(\dfrac{x_n}{y_n}\right)$ with $y_n \neq 0,\ \forall n \in \mathbb N$
> - $cX := (cx_n)$

## 3.2.3 Theorem: Convergence and algebra operations

> 1. Let $X = (x_n)$ and $Y = (y_n)$ be sequences of real numbers that converge to $x$ and $y$, respectively, and let $c \in \mathbb R$.
> 
> 	Then the sequences **$X + Y, X - Y, X \cdot Y$, and $cX$ converge to $x + y, x - y, xy$, and $cx$**, respectively.
> 2. If $X = (x_n)$ converges to $x$ and $Z = (z_n)$ is a sequence of nonzero real numbers that converges to $z$ and if $\lim(z_n) = z \neq 0$.
> 
> 	Then the quotient sequence **$X/Z$ converges to $x/z$**.

Notice the difference from the previous definition.

In this theorem, $X$ and $Y$ has to be convergent, so that the resulting limit is also convergent.

We use [[3 Reference/3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.10 Theorem Finding limit of a sequence\|Theorem 3.1.10]] to prove this theorem. See the example attached to that theorem.

## 3.2.4 Theorem: Limit of positive sequences

> If $X = (x_n)$ is a *convergent* sequence of real numbers
> and if *$x_n \geq 0$ for all $n \in \mathbb N$*,
> 
> then **$x = \lim(x_n) \geq 0$**.

$X$ is converging, and all its values are positive.

The limit must also be positive.

## 3.2.5 Theorem: Order preservation in limits of sequences

> If $X = (x_n)$ and $Y = (y_n)$ are *convergent* sequences of real numbers
> and if *$x_n \leq y_n$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$*,
> 
> then **$\lim(x_n) \leq \lim(y_n)$**.

In essence similar to [[3 Reference/3.2 Limit Theorems#3.2.4 Theorem Limit of positive sequences\|3.2.4]].

The difference is instead of comparing with $0$, we're comparing with another limit.

## 3.2.6 Theorem: Bounds of limit of sequences

> If $X=(x_n)$ is a *convergent* sequence
> and if *$a \leq x_n \leq b$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$*,
> 
> then **$a\leq\lim(x_n)\leq b$**.

Notion of [[3 Reference/2.3 The Completeness Property of R#2.3.1 Definition Bounded set\|bounds]] in converging sequences.

Extension of [[3 Reference/3.2 Limit Theorems#3.2.1 Definition Bounded sequence\|3.2.1]] and [[3 Reference/3.2 Limit Theorems#3.2.2 Theorem A converging sequence of real numbers is bounded\|3.2.2]]

## 3.2.7 Theorem: Squeeze theorem

> Suppose that $X=(x_n), Y=(y_n)$, and $Z=(z_n)$ are sequences of real numbers such that
> $$
> x_n \leq y_n \leq z_n, \quad \forall n\in\mathbb N
> $$
> and that *$\lim(x_n) = \lim(z_n)$*.
> 
> Then **$y = (y_n)$ is convergent** and
> **$$
> \lim(x_n) = \lim(y_n) = \lim(z_n)
> $$**

## 3.2.9 Theorem: Limit of absolute sequences

> Let the sequence $(x_n)$ *converge to $x$*.
> 
> Then the sequence **$(|x_n|)$ of absolute values converges to $|x|$**.
> That is, $x = \lim(x_n) \to |x| = \lim(|x_n|)$

From [[3 Reference/2.2 Absolute Value and the Real Line#2.2.3 Theorem Triangle inequality\|Triangle Inequality]].

## 3.2.10 Theorem: Limit of square root of sequences

> Let the sequence $(x_n)$ *converge to $x$*.
> and suppose that *$x_n\geq 0$*.
> 
> Then the sequence **$(\sqrt{x_n})$ of positive square roots converges to $\sqrt{ x }$**

## Proof

### 3.2.2

1. Premise
> Let $(x_n)$ be sequence of real numbers, and $\varepsilon := 1$
> Suppose that $\lim(x_n) =x$

2. From premise. Using [[3 Reference/3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.3 Definition Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence\|Definition 3.1.3]]
> Then there exists a natural number $K = K(1)$
> such that $|x_n - x| < 1$ for all $n \geq K$.

3. From 2. Using [[3 Reference/2.2 Absolute Value and the Real Line#2.2.3 Theorem Triangle inequality\|Triangle Inequality]]
> If we apply the Triangle Inequality with $n \geq K$, we obtain
> $$
> \textcolor{yellow}{|x_n|} = |x_n - x + x| \leq |x_n - x| + |x| \textcolor{yellow}{< 1 + |x|}
> $$

4. Define.
> If we set
> $$
> \textcolor{lime}M := \sup\set{|x_1|, |x_2|, \dots, |x_{K-1}|, \textcolor{yellow}{1 + |x|}},
> $$

5. From 3, 4.
> then it follows that $\textcolor{yellow}{|x_n|} \leq \textcolor{lime}M$ for all $n \in \mathbb N$.

> [!NOTE]
> Notice that in $\set{|x_1|, |x_2|, \dots, |x_{K-1}|}$ the subscripts are natural numbers.
> 
> Then it follows that the inequality applies for all $n\in\mathbb N$

### 3.2.4

Suppose the conclusion is not true and that $x < 0$;
then $\varepsilon := -x$ is positive.
Since $X$ converges to $x$,
there is $K\in\mathbb N$ such that
$$
x - \varepsilon < x_n < x + \varepsilon \qquad \forall n \geq K
$$
In particular, we have $x_K < x + \varepsilon = x + (-x) = 0$.
But this contradicts the hypothesis that $x_n \geq 0$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.
Therefore, this contradiction implies that $x\geq 0$.

### 3.2.5

Let $z_n := y_n - x_n$ so that $Z := (z_n) = Y - X$
and $z_n \geq 0$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.
It follows from Theorems 3.2.3 and 3.2.4 that
$$
0 \leq \lim Z = \lim(y_n) - \lim(x_n)
$$
so that $\lim(x_n) \leq \lim(y_n)$

### 3.2.6

Let $Y$ be the constant sequence $(b,b,b,\dots)$.
Theorem 3.2.5 implies that $\lim X \leq \lim Y = b$.
Similarly one shows that $a \leq \lim X$.

### 3.2.9

It follows from the Triangle Inequality that

$$
\left| |x_n| - |x| \right| \leq |x_n - x| \qquad \forall n \in \mathbb N
$$

Recall the definition of a [[3 Reference/3.1 Sequences and Their Limits#3.1.3 Definition Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence\|converging sequence]].

The convergence of $(|x_n|)$ to $|x|$ is then an immediate consequence of the convergence of $(x_n)$ to $x$.
