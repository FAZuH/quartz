## Definition

Let

- $V$ : Finite dimensional [[def-vector-space-axioms_202509240715|vector space]]
- $S={ \mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{v}\_{n} }$ : [[def-set_202510022014|Set]] of vectors in $V$

If

- $S$ [[def-span_202509240805|spans]] $V$
- $S$ is [[def-linear-independence_202510060445|linearly independent]]

Then we say $S$ is **basis** for $V$

## Remark

The difference between a $S$ being a basis or $V$ as opposed to simply spanning $V$ is the <u>additional condition that $S$ is linearly independent</u>, i.e., no vectors in $S$ can be expressed as a [[def-linear-combination_202510020342|linear combination]] as other vectors in $S$.

In other words, Suppose $S$ spans $V$. If $S$ is linearly independent then $S$ is basis for $V$.

## Finding basis for a vector space

Let

- $V$ : Finite dimensional vector space (e.g., $R^2$, $R^3$)
- $S=( \mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{v}\_{n} )$ : Set of vectors in $V$
- $\mathbf{c}=(c\_{1},c\_{2},\dots,c\_{n})$ : [[def-linear-combination_202510020342|Coefficient]]

1. Prove $S$ is linearly independent
   - Show that the vector equation $S\mathbf{c}^T=c\_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}+\dots+c_{n}\mathbf{v}\_{n}=\mathbf{0}$ has ONLY the [[Def-trivial-solution|trivial solution]].
2. Prove $S$ spans $V$
   - Show that the every vector $\mathbf{b}=(b\_{1},b\_{2},\dots,b\_{r})$ in $V$ can be expressed as $S\mathbf{c}^T=c\_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+c_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}+\dots+c_{n}\mathbf{v}\_{n}=\mathbf{b}$.

We can prove both point 1 and 2 by showing the matrix $A=S\mathbf{c}^T$ has a non 0 determinant ($\det(A)\neq 0$) by using statements (b), (e), and (g) of [[theorem-matrix-equivalency-statements_202510061112|matrix equivalency statements theorem]]. That is,

- (b) $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ has only the [[Def-trivial-solution|trivial solution]]
- (e) $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ is [[Def-consistency-of-linear-system|consistent]] for every $n\times 1$ matrix $\mathbf{b}$
- (g) $\det(A) \neq 0$

## Example: Basis for $R^n$

Let

- $\mathbf{v}\_{1}=(1,2,1)$
- $\mathbf{v}\_{2}=(2,9,0)$
- $\mathbf{v}\_{3}=(3,3,4)$

Following the procedure described in [[#Finding basis for a vector space]], our problem is now reduced into showing that the determinants of the following matrix is not 0:
$$
\det\left(\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 & 1 \\
2 & 9 & 0 \\
3 & 3 & 4
\end{bmatrix}\right) = -1 \neq 0
$$

Therefore, the set of vectors $S={ \mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2},\mathbf{v}\_{3} }$ is a basis for the vector space $R^3$.
