## Definition

Let:

- $W$ : A whole composed of parts
- $p_i$ : A part of $W$
- $P$ : A property

Then an argument commits the **Composition Fallacy** if and only if it asserts:

$$
\forall p_i \in W, \ p_i \text{ has property } P \implies W \text{ has property } P
$$

> [!NOTE]
> Properties of individual parts do not necessarily apply to the whole.

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: The Billionaire Argument
> **Scenario:** "Each member of our country is wealthy, therefore our country is wealthy."
>
> **Why it fails:** A country could have many moderately wealthy individuals but still have massive national debt or economic problems. Individual wealth doesn't determine national economic health.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: Sports Team Logic
> **Scenario:** "Each player on the team is the best in their position, so this must be the best team."
>
> **Why it fails:** Having individual stars doesn't guarantee team success. Teamwork, strategy, chemistry, and coaching matter. Many all-star teams have failed against cohesive units.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: The Atom Fallacy
> **Scenario:** "Atoms are invisible, therefore anything made of atoms is invisible."

**Why it fails:** Individual atoms are too small to see, but collections of trillions of atoms (like human bodies) are clearly visible. Emergent properties differ from component properties.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: Intelligent Individuals, Intelligent Group
> **Scenario:** "Everyone in this committee has a high IQ, so the committee will make smart decisions."
>
> **Why it fails:** Group dynamics, conformity pressures, and poor processes can lead intelligent individuals to make collectively poor decisions. Group intelligence isn't simply additive.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: The Brick House
> **Scenario:** "Each brick in this wall is small and light, therefore the wall is small and light."
>
> **Why it fails:** A wall made of many small bricks can be massive and heavy. The property of being "small" applies to individual bricks but not to the aggregate structure.
