## Definition

Let:

- $W$ : A whole composed of parts
- $p_i$ : A part of $W$
- $P$ : A property

Then an argument commits the **Division Fallacy** if and only if it asserts:

$$
W \text{ has property } P \implies \forall p_i \in W, \ p_i \text{ has property } P
$$

> [!NOTE]
> Properties of the whole do not necessarily apply to individual parts.

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: The Wealthy Nation Fallacy
> **Scenario:** "The United States is a wealthy country, so every American must be wealthy."
>
> **Why it fails:** National wealth averages mask huge individual disparities. A country can be economically prosperous while having citizens in poverty.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: Team Success
> **Scenario:** "This is the best basketball team in the league, so every player on the team must be the best."
>
> **Why it fails:** Teams succeed through synergy, strategy, and role specialization. A team can be great with role players who excel in specific functions rather than being individually superior.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: The Watchmaker's Argument
> **Scenario:** "This watch is heavy, therefore each component of the watch must be heavy."
>
> **Why it fails:** The property of weight distributes across components, but other properties (function, complexity, material) don't necessarily transfer from whole to parts.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: Corporate Blame
> **Scenario:** "That corporation committed fraud, so every employee is a criminal."
>
> **Why it fails:** Organizational misconduct often involves specific individuals or departments. Blaming all employees for decisions they may not have known about or supported is unfair.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: The Species Characteristic
> **Scenario:** "Humans are the most intelligent species, so every human is more intelligent than every member of other species."
>
> **Why it fails:** Species-level averages don't determine individual capabilities. Some animals demonstrate problem-solving abilities exceeding those of some humans.
