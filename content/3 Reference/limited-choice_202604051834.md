## Definition

Let:

- $P$ : A proposition that is false
- $Q$ : An alternative proposition

Then an argument commits the **Limited Choice** fallacy if and only if it asserts:

$$
P \text{ is false} \implies \text{Only } Q \text{ can be true}
$$

> [!NOTE]
> This fallacy ignores other possible alternatives beyond $Q$.

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: The Restaurant Dilemma
> **Scenario:** "You don't want pizza, so you must want burgers."
>
> **Why it fails:** There are countless other food options—sushi, tacos, salad, sandwiches, pasta, etc. Reducing choices to just two ignores the full menu of alternatives.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: The Educational Path
> **Scenario:** "If you don't go to university, you'll end up working minimum wage jobs forever."
>
> **Why it fails:** Many successful paths exist: trade schools, apprenticeships, entrepreneurship, military service, community college, online learning, and direct entry into various industries.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: The Investment Options
> **Scenario:** An advisor presents only high-risk stocks or low-yield savings accounts, ignoring bonds, index funds, real estate, or balanced portfolios.
>
> **Why it fails:** The financial landscape includes many middle-ground options. Presenting only extremes forces a choice between unnecessary risk and insufficient returns.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: The Transportation Choice
> **Scenario:** "You either drive a car everywhere or you take public transportation. Those are the only options."
>
> **Why it fails:** Walking, cycling, carpooling, ride-sharing, electric scooters, motorcycles, and combinations of these methods are all valid alternatives.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: The Political Candidate
> **Scenario:** "If you don't vote for Candidate A, you're effectively voting for Candidate B."
>
> **Why it fails:** Voters can choose third-party candidates, write-in candidates, or abstain entirely. Each choice has different implications, not just a binary transfer of votes.
