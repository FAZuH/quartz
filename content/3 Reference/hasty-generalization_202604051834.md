## Definition

Let:
- $A$ : An observed pattern or event
- $B$ : Another observed pattern or event

Then an argument commits the **Hasty Generalization** if and only if it asserts:
$$
A \text{ correlated with } B \implies A \text{ caused } B
$$

> [!NOTE]
> This fallacy confuses correlation with causation without sufficient evidence.

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: The One Bad Experience
> **Scenario:** After having one rude waiter at a restaurant, someone declares "All the service at that place is terrible, I'll never go back."
>
> **Why it fails:** One data point is insufficient to judge an entire establishment. The sample size is too small, and the conclusion is disproportionate to the evidence.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: Stereotyping
> **Scenario:** Meeting two people from a particular country who are loud, someone concludes "People from that country are always loud and obnoxious."
>
> **Why it fails:** Two individuals don't represent millions of people. This generalization ignores individual variation and creates harmful stereotypes based on minimal evidence.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: The Survey of Friends
> **Scenario:** A person asks three friends about their opinion on a political issue and concludes that the majority of the population agrees with them.
>
> **Why it fails:** Friends often share similar backgrounds and views. Three people who likely think alike don't represent the broader population's diversity of opinion.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: The Product Review
> **Scenario:** Someone buys a defective product and writes a review saying "This brand is garbage, never buy anything from them."
>
> **Why it fails:** One defective unit could be an outlier or shipping damage. Drawing conclusions about the entire brand from a single data point is unjustified.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: The Office Observation
> **Scenario:** Noticing two people leaving work early, a manager concludes that "Nobody here works a full day anymore" and implements strict attendance policies.
>
> **Why it fails:** Two instances don't establish a pattern, especially without context (perhaps they had appointments). Generalizing from limited observation leads to overreaction.
