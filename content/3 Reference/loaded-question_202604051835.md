## Definition

Let:

- $Q$ : A question
- $A$ : An unjustified assumption embedded in $Q$

Then an argument commits the **Loaded Question** fallacy if and only if:

$$
Q \text{ contains assumption } A \text{ without justification}
$$

> [!TIP] Interpretation
> The question is framed such that any answer implicitly accepts the unjustified assumption.

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: The Criminal Assumption
> **Scenario:** "When did you stop cheating on your spouse?" asks a lawyer in a divorce proceeding.
>
> **Why it fails:** Whether the person answers "I stopped last year" or "I never stopped," they implicitly admit to having cheated. The question assumes guilt that hasn't been established.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: The Abandonment Question
> **Scenario:** "Why do you hate America?" asked of someone who criticizes a specific policy.
>
> **Why it fails:** The question assumes the critic hates America rather than simply disagreeing with a policy. Any explanation offered accepts the false premise of hatred.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: The Have You Stopped
> **Scenario:** "Have you stopped making basic mistakes in your work?" asked by a supervisor during a performance review.
>
> **Why it fails:** Answering "yes" admits to past mistakes; answering "no" admits to current mistakes. The question assumes the employee makes basic mistakes regardless of the answer.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: The Religious Assumption
> **Scenario:** "What church do you go to?" asked by someone assuming the person is Christian.
>
> **Why it fails:** The question presumes religious affiliation and a specific type. Answering requires either accepting the assumption or awkwardly correcting the premise.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: The Incompetence Frame
> **Scenario:** "Why are you so bad at managing your time?" asked when someone is slightly late once.
>
> **Why it fails:** One instance of lateness doesn't establish a pattern of poor time management. The question assumes incompetence that a single data point doesn't support.
