## Definition

Let:
- $A$ : An event that occurred first
- $B$ : An event that occurred after $A$

Then an argument commits the **False Cause** fallacy if and only if it asserts:
$$
A \text{ came before } B \implies A \text{ caused } B
$$

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: The Rooster Fallacy
> **Scenario:** "The rooster crows every morning before sunrise, therefore the rooster's crowing causes the sun to rise."
>
> **Why it fails:** While the crowing consistently precedes sunrise, it's not the cause. Both are effects of the Earth's rotation and time of day, not causally related to each other.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: Superstition and Sports
> **Scenario:** An athlete wears lucky socks, wins a game, and concludes the socks caused the victory. They continue wearing them for every game.
>
> **Why it fails:** The victory likely resulted from skill, practice, and team performance. The temporal coincidence of wearing socks and winning doesn't establish causation.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: The Ice Cream and Drowning Correlation
> **Scenario:** "Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both increase in summer, so eating ice cream must cause drowning."
>
> **Why it fails:** Both increase because of a third factor—hot weather leads to more swimming and more ice cream consumption. They're correlated, not causally linked.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: The Curse of the Pharaohs
> **Scenario:** After opening King Tut's tomb, several expedition members died, leading to claims that the pharaoh's curse killed them.
>
> **Why it fails:** Deaths occurred years apart from various causes. Without comparing to death rates among similar populations and accounting for exposure to ancient pathogens, causation isn't established.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: Technology and Decline
> **Scenario:** "Society has gotten worse since smartphones were invented, so smartphones must be causing social decline."
>
> **Why it fails:** Many factors change over time simultaneously. Identifying one temporal correlation doesn't establish causation without controlling for other variables and mechanisms.
