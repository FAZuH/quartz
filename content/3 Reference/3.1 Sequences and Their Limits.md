- $X_{m}$: $m$-tail of $X$

1. $X$ convergent -> has exactly one limit
2. $X$ converges to $x$

## 3.1.1 Definition: Sequence of real numbers

> A **sequence of real numbers** (or sequence in $\mathbb R$) is
> a function with domain $\mathbb N$ and codomain $\mathbb R$.

In other words, a sequence in $\mathbb R$ assigns to each $n\in\mathbb N$ a uniquely determined real number. It is usually denoted as:
$
	X, \quad (x_n) \quad (x_n : n \in \mathbb N)
	$

The values of $x_n$ are called **$n$th-term** or **elements** of the sequence.

A sequence can also be defined by:

1. **Formula** of $n$-th term:

$$
X := \left( \frac{1}{2n} : n \in \mathbb N \right)
$$

2. List the terms **sequentially**, until the pattern seems evident:

$$
X := \left( \frac 1 2, \frac 1 4, \frac 1 6, \frac 1 8, \dots \right)
$$

3. **Inductively**/recursively

$$
x_1 := 2, \qquad x_{n+1} := x_n + 2
$$

4. **Constant** sequence. e.g., $(0, 0, 0, \dots)$.

## 3.1.3 Definition: Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence

> A sequence $(x_n)$ in $\mathbb R$ is said to:
>
> - **Converge** to $x\in\mathbb R$ or
> - $x$ be a **limit** of $(x_n)$
>
> if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a $K(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb N$ such that for all $n \geq K(\varepsilon)$,
> the terms $x_n$ satisfy _$|x_n - x| < \varepsilon$_.

> If a sequence has a limit, we say it's **convergent**. Otherwise, we say it's **divergent**.

## 3.1.8 Definition: Tail of a sequence

> If $X = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n, \dots)$ is a sequence of real numbers
> and if $m$ is a given natural number,
>
> then then the **$m$-tail** of $X$ is the sequence
>
> $$
> X_m := (x_{m + n} : n \in \mathbb N) = (x_{m + 1}, x_{m + 2}, \dots)
> $$

For example, the 3-tail of the sequence $X = (2,4,6,8,10, \dots, 2n, \dots)$, is the sequence $X_3 = (8, 10, 12, \dots, 2n + 6, \dots)$.

## 3.1.4 Theorem: Uniqueness of limit

> A **sequence in $\mathbb R$ has at most one limit**.

$\left[\lim(x_n) = x' \wedge \lim(x_n) = x''\right] \to x'=x''$

## 3.1.5 Theorem: Sequence limit equivalency

> Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence of real numbers, and let _$x \in \mathbb R$_.
> The following statements are equivalent:
>
> 1. $X$ converges to $x$.
> 2. $\forall \varepsilon > 0$, $\exists K \in \mathbb N$ such that $\forall n \geq K$, the terms $x_n$ satisfy $|x_n - x| < \varepsilon$.
> 3. $\forall \varepsilon > 0$, $\exists K \in \mathbb N$ such that $\forall n \geq K$, the terms $x_n$ satisfy $x - \varepsilon < x_n < x + \varepsilon$.
> 4. $\forall \varepsilon$-[neighborhood](2.2%20Absolute%20Value%20and%20the%20Real%20Line.md#2.2.7%20Definition%20Neighborhood) $V_\varepsilon(x)$ of $x$, $\exists K \in \mathbb N$ such that $\forall n \geq K$, the terms $x_n$ belong to $V_\varepsilon(x)$.

## 3.1.9 Theorem: Limit of a tail

> Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence of real numbers, and _$m \in \mathbb N$_.
>
> Then the **$m$-tail $X_m = (x_{m + n} : n \in \mathbb N)$ of $X$ converges if and only if $X$ converges**.
>
> In this case, $\lim X_m = \lim X$.

## 3.1.10 Theorem: Finding limit of a sequence

> Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence of real numbers and let $x \in \mathbb R$.
> If
>
> - $(a_n)$ is a sequence of _positive_ real numbers
> - _$\lim(a_n) = 0$_
> - For some constant _$C>0$_ and _$m\in\mathbb N$_ we have
>   \*\$\$
>   |x\_n - x| \leq Ca\_n\qquad\forall n\geq m
>   $$*
>   $$
>
> then it follows that **$\lim(x_n) = x$**.

> [!TIP] Interpretation
>
> We are basically saying:
>
> For a positive constant $C$,
> there exists the term $m\in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all terms $n \geq m$,
> the difference between $x_{n}$ and $x$ is less than $Ca_{n}$.
>
> And because $(a_{n}) \to 0$, it follows that $|x_{n} - x| \to 0$,
> which implies that $(x_{n}) \to x$

## Proofs

### 3.1.4

1. Premise

> Suppose that $x'$ and $x''$ are both limits of $(x_n)$.

2. From 1, [[#3.1.3 Definition Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence]]

> For each $\varepsilon > 0$,
>
> - there exists $K'$ such that $|x_n - x'| < \varepsilon/2$ for all $n \geq K'$ , and
> - there exists $K''$ such that $|x_n - x''| < \varepsilon/2$ for all $n \geq K''$.

3. Premise

> Let $K$ be the larger of $K'$ and $K''$.
> i.e., $K = \sup\{ K', K'' \}$

4. Obtain $|x' - x''| = 0$ using [[2.2 Absolute Value and the Real Line#2.2.3 Theorem Triangle inequality|Triangle Inequality]] and [[2.1 The Algebraic and Order Properties of R#2.1.9 Theorem Notion of limit from order|Theorem: Notion of limit from order]].

> For $n \geq K$, we apply triangle inequality to get
>
> $$
> \begin{align}
> |x' - x'' | &= | x' - x_n + x_n - x'' |\\
> &\leq |x' - x_n| + |x_n - x''| < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon
> \end{align}
> $$
>
> Since $\varepsilon > 0$ is an arbitrary positive number, we conclude that $|x' - x''| = 0$.

5. Conclusion

> Because $|x' - x''| = 0$, we can say that $x' = x''$.

> [!NOTE]
> Step 3 is needed because if $K$ is larger than $K'$ and $K''$,
>
> The 2 statements on step 2 applies for all $n \geq K$ (because $K > K'$ and $K > K''$.

### 3.1.5

Points 1. and 2. are just the definition.

Point 3 is obtained using [[2.2 Absolute Value and the Real Line#2.2.2 Theorem Additional property of absolute values|Theorem 2.2.2 (3)]].

$$
\begin{align}
|&x_n - x| < \varepsilon\\
- \varepsilon <\ &x_n - x < \varepsilon\\
x - \varepsilon <\ &x_n - x < x + \varepsilon
\end{align}
$$

Point 4 is obtained using [[2.2 Absolute Value and the Real Line#2.2.7 Definition Neighborhood|Definition 2.2.7: Neighborhood]].
We are basically saying $x_n$ is "close" to $x$.

### 3.1.9

1. From [[#3.1.8 Definition Tail of a sequence]]

> Note that for any $p \in \mathbb N$, the $p$-th term of $X_m$ is the $(p + m)$-th term of $X$.
> Similarly, if $q >m$, then the $q$-th term of $X$ is the $(q-m)$-th term of $X_m$. ^a

2. Premise

> Assume $X$ converges to $X$.

3. [[#3.1.3 Definition Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence]]

> Then given any $\varepsilon > 0$,
> if the terms of $X$ for $n \geq K(\varepsilon)$ satisfy $|x_n - x| < \varepsilon$,

4. From 1.

> then the terms of $X_m$ for $k \geq K(\varepsilon) - m$ satisfy $|x_k - x| < \varepsilon$.
> Thus we can take $K_m(\varepsilon) = K(\varepsilon) - m$, so that $X_m$ also converges to $x$.

5. Same as previous 2 steps

> Conversely,
> if the terms of $X_m$ for $k \geq K_m(\varepsilon)$ satisfy $|x_k - x| < \varepsilon$,
> then the terms of $X$ for $n \geq K(\varepsilon) + m$ satisfy $|x_n - x| < \varepsilon$.
> Thus we can take $K(\varepsilon) = K_m(\varepsilon) + m$.

6. Conclusion

> Therefore, $X$ converges to $x$ if and only if $X_m$ converges to $x$.

### 3.1.10

1. Premise

> - $(a_n)$ sequence of positive real numbers
> - $\lim(a_n) = 0$
> - $C > 0$, $m \in \mathbb N$
> - $|x_n - x| \leq C a_n, \qquad \forall n \geq m$

2. From 1,

> $$$\begin{align}
> a_n &= |a_n| && \text{Premise 1}\\
> 	&= |a_n - 0| && (\text{Addition identity})\\
> 	&< \varepsilon && (\text{Definition of limit of a sequence})\\
> 	&< \varepsilon / C && (C > 0, \varepsilon > 0)
> \end{align}$$
> $$$

3. From premise,

> $$$\begin{align}
> |x_n - x| &\leq Ca_n && \text{Premise 4}\\
> &<C \left( \frac \varepsilon C \right) && a_n < \varepsilon / C\\
> &<\varepsilon
> \end{align}$$
> $$$

4. Conclusion using [3.1.3 Definition: Converging and diverging (limit) of a sequence](#3.1.3%20Definition%20Converging%20and%20diverging%20\(limit\)%20of%20a%20sequence)

> Since $\varepsilon > 0$ is arbitrary, we conclude that $x_n = \lim(x_n)$
