## Definition

Let:

- $P$ : A proposition

Then an argument commits the **Appeal to Popularity** if and only if it asserts:

$$
\text{Many people believe } P \text{ is true} \implies P \text{ is true}
$$

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: Bandwagon Marketing
> **Scenario:** An advertisement claims a product is the best because "millions of people can't be wrong" and it's the top-selling brand.
>
> **Why it fails:** Popularity doesn't determine truth or quality. Millions of people once believed the Earth was flat, and top-selling products can still be inferior to alternatives.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: Common Belief in Health
> **Scenario:** Someone argues that a health practice must be effective because "everyone does it" and has for generations.
>
> **Why it fails:** Widely practiced traditions can be ineffective or harmful (bloodletting was common medical practice for centuries). Evidence, not tradition, determines efficacy.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: Social Media Consensus
> **Scenario:** A viral social media post claims something is true, and people accept it because it has millions of likes and shares.
>
> **Why it fails:** The number of shares doesn't correlate with accuracy. Misinformation can spread faster than truth precisely because it's sensational, not because it's correct.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: Peer Pressure Fallacy
> **Scenario:** Teenagers pressure someone to try a dangerous activity with the argument that "everyone's doing it, so it must be okay."
>
> **Why it fails:** The number of participants doesn't make an activity safe or wise. Groups can collectively make poor decisions that individuals wouldn't make alone.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: Historical Consensus
> **Scenario:** Scientists in the past dismissed new theories because the majority of experts disagreed with them.
>
> **Why it fails:** Scientific truth isn't determined by voting. Many revolutionary ideas (heliocentrism, germ theory, relativity) were initially rejected by the majority but proved correct.
