## Definition

Let:

- $P$ : A claim or proposition
- $S$ : The person making claim $P$
- $\text{Hypocrite}(S, P)$ : $S$ acts inconsistently with $P$

Then an argument commits the **Tu Quoque** (You Too) fallacy if and only if it asserts:

$$
\text{Hypocrite}(S, P) \implies P \text{ is false}
$$

> [!NOTE]
> The hypocrisy of the person making the claim does not invalidate the claim itself.

## History

In Latin, "Tu Quoque" means "you also" or "you too".

When accused of something, the deflection is "well, you do it too." The name captures that exact move — pointing back at the accuser. It was a common rhetorical tactic in Roman legal and political debate, where deflecting to the opponent's own behavior was a way to neutralize an argument without actually addressing it.

## Examples

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 1: The Parent's Defense
> **Scenario:** A parent tells their child to eat vegetables. The child responds, "You don't always eat vegetables, so why should I?"
>
> **Why it fails:** The parent's inconsistent behavior doesn't change the nutritional value of vegetables. The advice is valid regardless of whether the parent follows it perfectly.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 2: The Climate Activist's Travel
> **Scenario:** Someone criticizes a climate activist for using air travel to attend conferences, arguing their message about reducing emissions is invalid.
>
> **Why it fails:** The activist's personal carbon footprint doesn't determine whether climate science is correct. The facts about climate change stand independently of any individual's behavior.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 3: The Corrupt Policeman
> **Scenario:** A corrupt officer arrests someone for theft. The thief argues the arrest is invalid because the officer takes bribes.
>
> **Why it fails:** While the officer's corruption is a separate problem, it doesn't change the fact that theft is illegal. Two wrongs don't make the theft right.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 4: The Unhealthy Doctor
> **Scenario:** An overweight doctor advises a patient to lose weight for health reasons. The patient dismisses the advice because the doctor doesn't follow it.
>
> **Why it fails:** The doctor's weight doesn't change the health risks of obesity. The medical advice is based on evidence, not the physician's personal habits.

> [!EXAMPLE] Example 5: The Adulterous Marriage Counselor
> **Scenario:** A marriage counselor who is having an affair gives advice to a couple. One spouse dismisses all advice saying, "Why should we listen to someone who can't keep their own vows?"
>
> **Why it fails:** While the counselor's behavior is ethically questionable, their professional knowledge about relationship dynamics and communication strategies may still be valid and helpful.
